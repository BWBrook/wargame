# Case 013: *Dragons of Glory*

**Status:** independent dossier complete; Barry’s personal evaluation and the corpus-wide synthesis remain deferred.  
**Primary object:** TSR product 9144, DL11 *Dragons of Glory*, copyright 1986 and advertised as shipping in January 1986.  
**Form:** a two-coalition, continent-scale fantasy hex-and-counter wargame that can also generate an alternate War of the Lance timeline for an AD&D campaign.  
**Base-game designers:** Douglas Niles and Tracy Raye Hickman.  
**Advanced rules:** “More Dragons of Glory,” by Niles and Hickman, published separately in *Dragon* 107 in March 1986.  
**Research dates:** 23–24 August 2026.  
**Source ledger:** [sources.md](sources.md).  
**Pre-research user disclosures:** quarantined in [barry-context.md](barry-context.md).

## 1. Scope, disclosure firewall, and the 1985/1986 problem

The primary object is the game as sold in DL11: the eight-page standard rulebook, sixteen-page campaign/source booklet, two joined maps, counter sheet, cover tables, and event material. The advanced rules in *Dragon* 107 belong to the same design lineage and are essential evidence, but they were not inside the product. They add and replace major systems rather than supplying a few harmless variants. Base and advanced play are therefore analysed separately before their interaction is assessed. [S01, S05]

Barry identified the title as the direct tabletop precursor to SSI’s *War of the Lance* and named its factions, neutral nations, leaders, and alternate-history campaign. Those statements set questions, not answers. The lineage was checked against both rule sets and the SSI manual; the mechanical importance of each named feature was independently reconstructed. Barry has not yet said whether he liked the game, how long he played it, or which features mattered to him. [S15–S16]

The printed title page says copyright 1986. TSR’s January 1986 issue of *Dragon* placed DL11 under “Titles Shipping in January,” and period reviews followed in June 1986 and October–November 1987. Several catalogues and a museum object record instead say 1985. The cleanest account is that 1985 was a production, catalogue, or early-product year retained by some databases, while the best-supported commercial-release window is early 1986. An exact first-sale day was not located. [S01–S03, S06–S08]

This matters because the official advanced system appeared in March 1986—very soon after the January shipping notice. It should not be described as a mature edition produced after years of developed play. It is closer to an immediate optional enlargement of a deliberately compressed design, accompanied by limited counter errata. [S02, S05]

## 2. Product identity and credited production

The title page calls *Dragons of Glory* an “Official Simulation Game,” not an AD&D adventure. Douglas Niles and Tracy Raye Hickman are credited as authors/designers. Mike Breault edited; Jeff Easley provided the cover; Jeff Butler and Diana Magnuson supplied interior art; David Sutherland III, Steven D. Sullivan, and Picard Didier Inc. handled cartography; Linda Bakk and Carolyn Vanderbilt typography; and Colleen O’Malley keylining. Its stock number is 9144 and ISBN 0-88038-094-2. [S01]

The physical package comprised:

- a stiff double cover with charts and game text inside;
- a large two-part full-colour hex map of Ansalon and surrounding islands;
- about 400 die-cut counters and a small resealable bag;
- an eight-page standard-rule booklet;
- a sixteen-page campaign, scenario, history, and order-of-battle booklet; and
- strategic-event information printed as part of the game material. [S01, S03, S06, S08]

The unusual “module” packaging produced an expectation problem from the start. *White Dwarf* opened its review with a warning that this was not another Dragonlance adventure. *Space Gamer/Fantasy Gamer* made the same clarification a year later. The product sat in the numbered DL adventure line but required no AD&D rules to function as a board wargame. Conversely, an AD&D group could use it without being obliged to play the strategic contest to completion. [S01, S06–S07]

## 3. The product was commissioned for an audience crossing

DL13’s later saga questionnaire says that many respondents to the earlier DL5 survey had expressed interest in a Dragonlance board game, and that TSR designed DL11 in response. The same questionnaire asks whether players enjoyed it, wanted more introductory board games, thought it should have been simpler or harder, disliked it, did not understand it, had not played or bought it, or did not want board games. [S04]

That is unusually revealing product-origin evidence. TSR was not merely selling a fantasy skin to established wargamers. It was attempting to bring at least part of the Dragonlance role-playing audience into strategic board play. The resulting design had to serve people who might recognise Laurana, Silvanesti, Neraka, and dragonlances but not zones of control, odds columns, step losses, or campaign setup. [S01, S04]

No survey results survive in the inspected material. It would therefore be wrong to claim that the crossing succeeded, that a large audience demanded it, or that TSR learned a clear lesson from purchasers. What survives is the ambition and the categories of possible response TSR considered important—including incomprehension and opposing demands for more and less difficulty. [S04]

## 4. Three scales in one campaign ecosystem

DL11’s prologue divides the Dragonlance war into three linked scales:

1. **character scale:** the AD&D heroes pursue the quest that can defeat the Queen of Darkness;
2. **medium battle scale:** BATTLESYSTEM can resolve skirmishes or battlefield actions involving player characters and assembled forces;
3. **continental scale:** *Dragons of Glory* moves national armies, fleets, flying forces, leaders, and political allegiances across Ansalon. [S01, S10, S17]

The board game is fully playable at the third scale without either of the first two. Its scenario booklet nevertheless includes military histories and force organisations that can generate BATTLESYSTEM statistics. A referee can also play the strategic game, record the evil armies’ locations over time, and use those positions to alter encounters, refugee flows, local troop presence, and battle news in an AD&D campaign. [S01]

This is not a conventional expansion in which one rules engine mechanically calls another. It is a **campaign ecosystem proposal**: events at different scales are meant to inform one another. The connection is conceptually strong and procedurally loose. The base rules offer dates, suggested correspondences, and examples, but not a complete protocol for translating a player-character victory into changed counters, or a strategic battle into a particular AD&D encounter. [S01, S09–S10]

## 5. Grand strategy by theatre and politics, not by economy

The game deserves “grand strategic” because it gives each player a coalition-wide role across the whole continent. The player decides which nations enter the war, where national armies concentrate, which coasts and sea routes matter, when flying forces risk deep operations, which fortresses must be held, and how the Highlord’s early expansion meets Whitestone’s later mobilisation. [S01]

It is not an economic grand-strategy game. There are no taxes, resource nodes, technology tree, generic construction queue, city development, or freely designed force mix. Most armies already exist as national contingents and enter through political activation. Casualties return through a replacement-pool procedure; the Highlord separately produces draconian armies at the Dark Temple. Strategic events introduce rare assets. [S01]

The player’s growth is therefore:

- **political:** turn a neutral country into an ally;
- **geographic:** acquire access, ports, fortresses, and routes;
- **military:** preserve and recombine national forces through depletion and replacement;
- **narrative:** receive dragonlances, dragons, wizards, citadels, famous leaders, or other special events. [S01]

That focus keeps administration small relative to the map and counter count. It also means that the campaign has few soft recovery levers. A player cannot compensate for a lost country by changing taxation, recruiting a new generic army, or investing in an alternative technology. Political activation, surviving force structure, event timing, and territorial collapse carry unusually high leverage. [S01]

## 6. Two campaigns and four scenarios

The base package contains two campaigns and four scenarios. The campaign pair serves different purposes:

| Mode | Start | Strategic condition | Intended use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Full Campaign | Battle Turn 1, March–April 348 AC | Highlord begins from Neraka with its core war machine; Whitestone begins with no activated national armies | Rewrite the war from its beginning |
| Historical Campaign | Battle Turn 20, winter 351 AC | Many Highlord conquests and Whitestone alliances are already established; Silvanesti is displaced and weakened | Enter near the adventure-module situation; easier for a referee to align with the role-playing saga |

The full campaign runs thirty battle turns. Ordinary turns represent two months; each winter turn represents November through February. The printed chart begins in March 348 AC and ends in the winter of 353 AC. The designers estimate six to eight hours for experienced players. The Historical Campaign begins late in the same track and is shorter but explicitly described as equally complex. [S01]

The four base scenarios are:

1. **The Fall of Silvanesti** — a focused invasion and defence;
2. **The Solamnic Plain** — a larger situation introducing limited activation;
3. **The Kender Strike** — the shortest and simplest introduction to movement and combat;
4. **The Siege of Kalaman** — a short, furious engagement using the full standard rules. [S01]

The scenario booklet orders them by fictional chronology, not learning order. The prologue recommends beginning with scenario three, then scenario one, then scenario two; scenario four is the compact all-rules contest. That is a thoughtful tutorial ladder hidden inside a less helpful table-of-contents sequence. A novice following page order rather than the prose recommendation encounters the wrong progression. [S01, S06]

The scenarios also do more than shorten commitment. They narrow geography, forces, event pools, and political procedure so the learner can isolate combat, then defence and offence, then activation, then the full system. The concept is pedagogically sound even though contemporary reviewers found setup and rules defects that damaged its execution. [S01, S06–S07]

## 7. Alternate history is structurally real

The full campaign is not a reenactment script. It begins before the Highlords’ historical conquests and before Whitestone has an army on the map. Neutral activation, invasion responses, initiative, combat, conquest, and staged event draws can create a substantially different political and military history. The referee is expressly invited to record that history and let it replace the novels’ background. [S01]

The Historical Campaign performs the opposite operation. It freezes a later canonical state: multiple Highlord countries are already active, Nordmaar and Kothas have fallen, Whitestone has assembled western and elven allies, and the displaced Silvanesti government survives on Southern Ergoth at reduced strength without its griffons. From there, the players can still diverge, but the first nineteen turns of political and military possibility have been removed. [S01]

This pair creates two kinds of authorship:

- **deep counterfactual:** who allies, who falls, and where the fronts form from 348 AC;
- **late divergence:** what happens after the saga’s familiar war state has already emerged. [S01]

The standard game supports alternate **military and political** history much better than alternate character history. Major story achievements enter mainly as random strategic events, not chosen quests. The advanced rules later try to return heroes and artefact-seeking to player control. [S01, S05]

## 8. The standard battle turn

A base-game battle turn contains eight steps:

1. replacements;
2. draw and apply a strategic event;
3. each side attempts to activate a neutral nation;
4. determine initiative;
5. the first side moves;
6. the first side resolves combat;
7. the second side moves and resolves combat;
8. advance the turn marker. [S01]

The sequence is short enough to hold in memory. Each step answers a different strategic question:

- What strength returns?
- What exceptional event changes the war?
- Which political actor enters?
- Who chooses the order of operations?
- Where does each coalition commit?
- Which combats are worth accepting now? [S01]

There is no separate production, technology, administration, morale, supply, or diplomacy panel in the standard game. Yet the turn can still be long because a whole coalition moves potentially hundreds of counters before its opponent acts. Procedural brevity and physical workload are not the same thing. [S01, S06]

## 9. Initiative is order control, not merely “who goes first”

Both players roll a four-sided die. A tie preserves the previous holder. The winner does not automatically move first; the winner chooses which side conducts the first movement/combat pair. [S01]

That choice matters in several ways:

- move first to seize an exposed fortress or launch an attack before reinforcement;
- force the opponent to reveal movement and then counter;
- arrange to move second in one turn and first in the next, creating a double operation across the turn boundary;
- preserve the previous initiative on a tie, making an earlier win persist probabilistically. [S01]

The mechanism is only one roll and one declaration, but it governs the tempo of every army. It creates suspense and operational reversals without a complex chit-pull or alternating-activation system. It can also magnify luck: a vulnerable front may face two enemy movement/combat sequences before responding. There is no local reaction system for ordinary ground armies in the base rules. [S01]

## 10. Neutral nations are the force-generation system

Each side may try to activate exactly one neutral country per turn. The player rolls a ten-sided die against the nation’s side-specific activation value. The table is sharply asymmetric: some countries are plausible Whitestone allies and effectively impossible Highlord recruits; others have the reverse inclination. Blode, for example, is strongly Highlord-facing, while Goodlund and Qualinesti favour Whitestone. [S01]

An activated nation contributes its complete designated force and changes access to its territory. Political allegiance is therefore simultaneously:

- reinforcement;
- movement permission;
- a new defensive obligation;
- control of cities, fortresses, ports, and coast;
- a changed route network;
- an additional country that can later be conquered. [S01]

The one-attempt limit creates opportunity cost. A player cannot efficiently “work” every neutral at once. A high-probability nearby ally competes with a low-probability state whose geography or unusual force could change the campaign. Because failure normally consumes the attempt, political tempo can diverge sharply between otherwise similar games. [S01]

Invasion forces the issue. When a player enters neutral territory—even by flying over it—the two sides make alternating activation attempts until the country joins someone, with an advantage to the non-invading coalition. Coastal sailing past a neutral does not count. This converts aggression into a political gamble: the Highlord may open a route or instead deliver the target and its armies to Whitestone. [S01]

The Knight realms contain a separate mobilisation clock. Their Whitestone activation chances improve as each nation is conquered by or allied to the Highlord. Activating the first Knight polity also deploys the High Clerist Tower armies. The coalition’s institutional centre becomes easier to mobilise as the threat becomes undeniable. [S01]

This is a compact model of political asymmetry. Nations are not negotiated with through dialogue, promises, influence points, or long tracks; a single attempt resolves entry. The strength is speed and map consequence. The cost is high variance and limited agency over *why* a country joins. [S01, S09]

## 11. Movement is a small rule set with unusual reach

Most ground movement is familiar. Forest and river terrain cost more; mountains, deep rivers, marshes, desert, and sea impose type-specific prohibitions. Elves and kender move efficiently in forests, while dwarves and ogres can cross mountains at additional cost. These racial exceptions make faction identity operational rather than merely decorative. [S01]

Stacking is deliberately tight:

- normally two ground armies per hex;
- three in a fortified city;
- up to two air armies;
- leaders and ships do not consume the ordinary ground limit;
- kender armies cannot stack with other armies. [S01]

The narrow limit prevents a single immense land pile from absorbing a whole front. Concentration instead comes from adjacent multi-hex attacks, reserves, leaders, air support, and special transport. Fortified cities have slightly greater capacity and therefore function as both defence and concentration points. [S01]

The base game uses a stopping rule rather than a fully elaborated zone-of-control system. A ground army entering a hex adjacent to an enemy normally stops. An army beginning adjacent must move out of all enemy adjacency; cavalry and leader-accompanied forces receive exemptions. The result makes contact “sticky” without a separate engagement marker. [S01]

Fantasy mobility deliberately breaks this front geometry:

- flying forces ignore most terrain and ordinary enemy adjacency, but must begin and end on land;
- griffons and pegasi can carry infantry of the same nationality, at reduced reach;
- ships have effectively unlimited movement along open sea and coast, limited river movement, and carry one ground army plus leaders;
- a stationary ship can serve as a river bridge;
- wizards can be picked up and placed with any friendly army or ship during movement;
- flying citadels can carry up to three Highlord armies over terrain. [S01]

The strategic map is therefore never only a land front. Islands, rivers, coastal fortresses, rear cities, and apparently protected capitals remain exposed to naval or airborne coups. Because conquest can collapse an entire country, these movement exceptions carry consequences much larger than the counter that makes the move. [S01]

## 12. The Maelstrom is a one-roll strategic story

Ships entering the Maelstrom roll a ten-sided die. One result sinks them, several hold them in place, several eject them through an exit chosen by the opponent, and the best results let their owner choose the exit. [S01]

This is a compact push-your-luck mechanism:

- risk destruction;
- risk losing tempo;
- risk strategic displacement controlled by the enemy;
- or gain extraordinary cross-map repositioning. [S01]

It does not simulate navigation in detail, but it turns a setting landmark into a strategic decision rather than printed lore. Because ships can carry armies and leaders, a single result can affect much more than naval strength. [S01]

## 13. Land and air combat: odds, steps, and modifiers

An attacking army strikes an adjacent defending hex after movement. Several attacking hexes may combine against one defender, but each defending hex is attacked only once and each army attacks only once in the phase. Players total combat strength, establish an odds column, roll a ten-sided die, apply modifiers, and read a combat-results table. [S01]

The principal modifiers include:

- one chosen leader’s tactical rating;
- dragons and opposing dragon counters;
- wizards;
- cavalry and flying participation;
- rivers, bridges, fords, and passes;
- cities, fortified cities, fortresses, and dwarven fortresses;
- racial advantages in national or favoured terrain; and
- special event assets. [S01]

Armies have a full-strength and depleted side. Results can deplete one or more units, deplete an entire force, force retreat, or eliminate units. The affected player often chooses which constituent army absorbs the result. Combat is therefore both a probability problem and a force-preservation decision: which nationality, movement type, special ability, or replacement prospect can be risked? [S01]

Successful attackers may advance into vacated ground. Eligible air and cavalry forces can then gain an additional hex, making them useful for turning a local result into a deeper positional breach. Cavalry and air defenders may evade attacks that lack corresponding mobile forces. [S01]

The base combat system is simple in procedure but not low-impact. A leader, dragon flight, wizard, river, or fortress can shift the d10 outcome strongly. Many simultaneous modifiers act on a result range extending below zero and well above ten. The player may understand the procedure quickly while still needing experience to value a battle. [S01, S07]

## 14. Dragons are force and combat shift at once

Dragons have double leverage. Their strength contributes when the odds ratio is calculated, and their presence also modifies the combat die roll. Against an unprepared ground stack, air superiority can therefore improve both the column and the result within the column. [S01]

Dragonlances are an equally strong countermeasure. The first event represents rediscovery of the craft; later counters represent production. An army carrying lances doubles its strength and denies an opposing dragon’s combat modifier against its stack. The counter is attached to a particular army and can be lost permanently with it. [S01]

The game’s war arc is embedded in this relation:

1. Highlord dragons provide early operational and combat dominance;
2. Whitestone survives, activates allies, and waits for narrative reversals;
3. dragonlances and good dragons can neutralise or reverse air power;
4. the location and survival of the empowered army become strategic objectives. [S01]

The standard game delivers this arc mainly through event draws rather than a player-controlled discovery process. It is dramatic and setting-faithful in broad outline, but the timing can swing a campaign and the player has little influence over when the required event emerges. [S01]

## 15. Strategic events compress the saga into a cup

One strategic event is drawn each turn, with the Highlord and Whitestone alternating responsibility by odd and even turns. Event groups enter in stages—E1, E10, and E20—so the possible history changes as the war matures. [S01]

The event set includes:

- activation bonuses;
- wizards;
- the discovery and later production of dragonlances;
- dragon orbs;
- Laurana as the Golden General;
- the arrival of good dragons;
- gnomish technology;
- flying citadels; and
- Lord Soth’s legion. [S01]

These events are not equivalent bonuses. A wizard teleports between friendly forces and shifts combat. A successful dragon-orb use can destroy opposing dragons, while failure can remove both orb and leader. Gnomish technology can produce a large favourable modifier or catastrophically backfire on doubles. A flying citadel can carry three armies, ignore terrain, strip defenders of terrain and fortress benefits when attacking, resist ground attack entirely, and defend against air as a fortified city. [S01]

The staged cup is an economical substitute for research, production, quest tracks, and scripted story chapters. It ensures that later-war capabilities do not normally appear at the opening and creates replay variation without a deck-building system. It also converts achievements performed by characters in the modules into exogenous strategic fortune. [S01]

The prologue acknowledges the mismatch. A referee aligning the board game with the adventure modules may introduce the orbs and first dragonlance at Turn 20 rather than leaving them wholly to random timing. The base game can therefore be played as a counterfactual simulation or partially constrained to reproduce the story. [S01]

## 16. Leaders are important numbers, not yet strategic characters

In the standard game, leaders usually:

- move with armies;
- supply one selected tactical modifier to a battle;
- sometimes affect naval combat;
- carry or use special items;
- risk death when a ship sinks. [S01]

That is thin treatment for a setting famous for individual heroes and villains. Tanis, Laurana, Verminaard, and the Highlords do not conduct chosen quests, persuade countries in person, duel, escape, or search the map under the base rules. Their identity is primarily a tactical rating and eligibility for exceptional effects. [S01, S05]

The standard rules also omit a general answer for a leader whose entire land stack is eliminated. *Dragon* 107 adds a die roll: the leader is either eliminated or placed with the nearest friendly force. This is a small erratum with a large thematic implication—one of the game’s signature character types had no complete base-game fate rule. [S05]

## 17. Naval combat is a separate attritional duel

Ships use their own combat procedure. Each ship rolls a d10 and scores if the result is at or below its combat rating, subject to limited leader and wizard effects. Results are simultaneous. One hit flips a ship to its damaged side; a second sinks it permanently. Rounds continue until one side withdraws or is destroyed. [S01]

When a transport sinks:

- the carried army enters its replacement pool rather than necessarily vanishing forever;
- a leader may die or wash up with the nearest friendly force;
- wizards escape. [S01]

The naval game is smaller than the land system but materially connected to it. Fleets move armies, bridge rivers, threaten islands and coastlines, and can die permanently rather than circulate through ordinary replacement. A naval loss can remove irreplaceable transport capacity even if the embarked ground army eventually returns. [S01]

The base rules do not allow interception during an enemy move. The advanced system later gives ships and flying units reaction range, making control of sea and air lanes less dependent on ending adjacent to an enemy. [S05]

## 18. Attrition without an economy

Eliminated armies normally go to a national replacement pool. During the replacement step, two eliminated counters of one nationality can produce one returning full-strength army while the other is permanently removed. Two depleted armies stacked together can similarly be consolidated: one goes to the pool and the other returns to full strength. [S01]

This creates a distinctive ablation model:

- the first loss does not necessarily erase a formation forever;
- repeated losses shrink the nation’s total force;
- national pools cannot freely substitute for one another;
- a player may combine two damaged formations to restore one effective counter;
- irreplaceable ships, dragons, griffons, and pegasi make some losses qualitatively different. [S01]

The system models manpower and organisational exhaustion without counting replacement points. It also rewards preserving pairs and national identity. One isolated casualty may wait uselessly in a pool until a second loss permits one return; two depleted armies must meet to consolidate. [S01]

The Highlord has an additional source of mass. Each replacement step can produce one draconian army at the Dark Temple, up to the available limit of twenty. Capturing the temple ends that production and its replacement function. This is the nearest the base game comes to a production centre, and it gives Whitestone a concrete reason to threaten the Highlord core beyond final victory. [S01]

## 19. Conquest turns rear-area raids into national collapse

A country is conquered when all of its fortresses and fortified cities are captured. Its land and air armies and leaders are then removed permanently, while its ships remain with the coalition they had joined. The Knight realms are federated: their forces survive until all the designated Knight sites and the High Clerist Tower have fallen. [S01]

Cities and fortresses have no automatic garrison. A player must leave counters behind if a key site is to resist. Because flying and naval forces can cross ordinary front geometry, a lightly held rear fortress is a real vulnerability. A small airborne or amphibious operation can trigger the loss of every army belonging to a country, even if those armies are winning elsewhere. [S01]

This makes rear defence meaningful without supply counters or economic production. It also creates a severe positive-feedback mechanism:

1. lose a key site;
2. lose a nation’s active field armies and leaders;
3. open more territory and objectives;
4. face the next attack with fewer forces;
5. risk another national collapse. [S01]

An optional rule allows elves and kender to disband and later re-establish a mobile capital after a random delay. It represents refugee government and survival outside the original homeland. *White Dwarf* identified a scenario exploit: in *The Fall of Silvanesti*, the defending elves can apparently disband the very capital the Highlord must capture, frustrating the victory condition. [S01, S06]

That example is important. It is not simply a dislike of abstraction. It is a direct collision between an optional campaign rule and a specific scenario objective, left unresolved in the short rules. [S06]

## 20. Kender taunting is a national ability with positional teeth

After enemy movement and before ordinary attacks, a kender army may taunt an adjacent enemy stack. On a successful d6 result, that stack must attack immediately, alone, and with a penalty; other stacks cannot join it. [S01]

This makes the kender’s fictional temperament mechanically legible:

- use a weak or awkwardly placed kender force as bait;
- break a carefully prepared combined attack into an isolated assault;
- force an enemy to attack poor terrain or a supported defender;
- trigger combat before the opponent’s chosen sequence. [S01]

The ability is memorable because it changes the opponent’s action rather than merely adding strength. It is also swingy and proximity-dependent. Kender cannot stack normally, so they must expose themselves to use it. [S01]

## 21. The standard asymmetry

The two coalitions do not begin as equivalent colour swaps.

### Dragon Highlords

- begin the full campaign with the only activated core;
- possess dragons and concentrated offensive capacity;
- can produce draconians at the Dark Temple;
- can invade neutrals to accelerate political resolution;
- gain flying citadels and Lord Soth through later events;
- seek to conquer the Knight institutional network before Whitestone fully mobilises. [S01]

### Whitestone

- begins the full campaign without active national armies;
- builds a coalition one activation at a time;
- receives threat-driven improvement in Knight mobilisation;
- depends heavily on preserving countries and time;
- can gain the Golden General, good dragons, dragonlances, and other reversal assets;
- eventually seeks to carry the war into Neraka. [S01]

The asymmetry is temporal as much as numerical. Highlord begins with agency, concentration, and air dominance. Whitestone begins with political waiting and defensive risk but has a widening coalition and stronger late narrative counters. [S01]

The game therefore creates a recognisable campaign arc without a scripted sequence of mandatory conquests. Whether the arc appears at a satisfying tempo depends on activation rolls, initiative, event order, and early combat. Those same variables can instead make the opening feel like a runaway or make Whitestone feel inactive. [S01, S09]

## 22. Victory makes the war territorial but graded

The full campaign ends after thirty turns and assesses qualifying countries controlled by each side. The rulebook gives graded outcomes rather than only win/loss, allowing a side’s territorial position to be described from decisive success through poor or indecisive performance. Countries with no relevant armies unless activated are treated specially in the count. [S01]

This victory model fits the standard game’s level of abstraction. There is no economic score or technology prestige; the war is about which coalition has brought the political map under control. The scenarios use local objectives and time limits instead. [S01]

The prologue adds a second campaign-level logic when DL11 is used with AD&D: Whitestone can win militarily, the role-playing heroes can complete the quest, and the Queen of Darkness succeeds if neither happens. The board game alone cannot resolve the quest victory. That outcome exists only when two campaign layers are actually linked. [S01]

## 23. “More Dragons of Glory” changes the design’s centre of gravity

The March 1986 article states the trade-off directly. The base game was designed to move quickly, and details affecting combat were omitted for ease of play. The advanced rules are offered to players wanting more realism at the cost of somewhat greater complexity. [S05]

They change the standard turn from approximately two months to approximately one month, with winter representing three months, and replace the eight-step sequence with:

1. replacements and artefact placement;
2. declare active heroes;
3. perform heroic actions;
4. initiative;
5. first movement;
6. first combat;
7. second movement and combat;
8. supply;
9. turn marker and end. [S05]

The article adds:

- on-map heroes;
- active-hero limits;
- diplomacy that can activate, deactivate, or reverse a country;
- searching for and uncovering artefacts;
- special heroic actions;
- personal combat, capture, escape, healing, and item transfer;
- command radius and racial command restrictions;
- air and naval interception;
- detailed winter;
- supply;
- dragon attacks on ships;
- three additional scenarios; and
- counter and leader-fate errata. [S05]

This is not just “more chrome.” It moves agency away from random activation and event draws toward a small number of highly empowered characters, while adding logistical and command constraints to the army layer. [S05]

## 24. Heroes turn story achievements into player actions

Heroes enter the advanced game from Turn 10. Whitestone receives the familiar Heroes of the Lance; starting Highlords count as hero-leaders, and activated Knight leaders can fill the same role for Whitestone. Each side designates one active hero per turn, and that character performs one heroic action. [S05]

The one-active-hero limit is a powerful compression. Instead of moving every named character through a separate adventure phase, the player chooses which exceptional intervention defines the turn. The options include:

- attempt diplomacy;
- search for an artefact;
- uncover an artefact after satisfying its condition;
- create a diversion;
- pursue the Hammer of Kharas;
- make a valiant sacrifice;
- unite the Whitestone Council;
- impose Highlord martial law;
- spread distrust; or
- employ spies. [S05]

Artefacts use a two-stage structure. A hero first locates a hidden marker, then draws an item, and then must satisfy that specific item’s condition to uncover its effect. Items move with heroes, leaders, armies, or ships and can be exchanged, captured, or lost through personal combat. [S05]

This solves one base-game problem: the player now does something recognisably heroic to obtain narrative power. It also creates more procedure and more extreme outcomes. A single diplomacy check can deactivate a whole nation or reverse its alliance; a heroic attempt can kill the character; an artefact condition may be impossible within a scenario and requires a special substitute procedure. [S05]

## 25. Advanced diplomacy is more agentic and more volatile

The standard country system uses side-specific activation numbers and one abstract attempt per turn. The advanced rules remove the ordinary activation-bonus counters and use on-map hero diplomacy instead. The active hero must reach the relevant neutral leader and roll against a diplomatic table, modified by character and national relations. [S05]

The acting player declares one of three goals:

- activate a neutral;
- deactivate an opposing ally;
- change an existing alliance. [S05]

The gain in agency is substantial. Geography, hero movement, capture risk, and diplomatic capability now matter. Political change is something a player stages rather than a roll made from nowhere. [S05]

The leverage is also substantial. A successful check can remove or turn an entire national force. That may produce a wonderful alternate-history reversal, but it can also make a single hero roll outweigh several turns of military manoeuvre. The advanced game substitutes dramatic character agency for the base game’s impersonal activation variance; it does not eliminate high political variance. [S05]

## 26. Command radius makes the coalition hard to coordinate

In the advanced rules a leader’s tactical rating also becomes a radius in hexes. An army must begin movement or combat within an eligible leader’s radius to act. Racial and national restrictions determine which troops will accept which commander; draconians are more flexible, while elves and other proud peoples resist outside command. [S05]

This creates a second map behind the visible front:

- where can a leader project action?
- which armies accept that leader?
- can a multinational attack be assembled under one command?
- does a fast advance move beyond next turn’s control?
- can capture or death paralyse a sector? [S05]

The rule turns leaders from combat modifiers into operational infrastructure. It also adds considerable checking. Command is evaluated at the beginning of the relevant step, and different national exceptions must be remembered across a map with many contingents. [S05]

## 27. Advanced supply is severe but contains a loophole

Only stacks containing more than one ground army must trace supply. A lone army is assumed to forage. A required supply line may be any length through connected land hexes to a friendly fortress or fortified city, but enemy forces, hostile control, and mountains outside passes can block it. During the supply step, an unsupplied stack loses an army to the replacement pool. [S05]

This adds strategic value to:

- fortresses and fortified cities beyond conquest;
- passes and chokepoints;
- cutting a multinational concentration off from its rear;
- winter pass closure;
- dispersing forces before a supply check. [S05]

The last point is also the rule’s weakness. Splitting a two-army unsupplied stack into separate hexes converts both armies into self-foraging units, if movement and space permit. A rule meant to constrain large formations can reward gamey dispersal immediately before the supply step. [S05]

The abstraction may be read charitably as an attempt to distinguish the burden of concentrated armies from small raiding forces. But because the threshold is binary and visible, it creates a strong manipulation opportunity. [S05]

## 28. Interception and winter make the opponent present during movement

Under advanced interception, when a ship or air army enters within six hexes of an eligible enemy ship or air force, the non-moving player may attempt to react. A d6 succeeds if it equals or exceeds range. A successful interceptor moves adjacent, immediately attacks, and then returns; each potential interceptor may try once in the enemy movement step. [S05]

This changes air and naval positioning from endpoint control to **threat coverage**. A fleet or dragon flight influences a radius even when it never moves in its owner’s turn. Routes become corridors through overlapping risks, and the moving player can be intercepted multiple times by different forces. [S05]

Winter adds:

- a penalty that reduces interception reach;
- frozen rivers that ground armies can cross while ships cannot use them;
- a one-in-six sinking risk for each moving ship;
- closed mountain passes that also block supply;
- repeated grounding checks for air forces after four, eight, twelve, and sixteen hexes. [S05]

The winter system creates seasonal geography rather than merely a longer calendar turn. Rivers become land routes and naval barriers; passes shut; long-range air operations become hazardous. It is much richer than the base game’s treatment, but also requires repeated checks and exception handling. [S05]

## 29. The advanced scenarios are system demonstrations

*Dragon* 107 adds:

- **The Invasion of Abanasinia / The Qualinesti War**, highlighting heroes, artefacts, and an early campaign;
- **The Maelstrom Fleet Action**, focusing on naval movement and interception;
- **The Battle for Neraka**, a late-war all-systems confrontation. [S05]

These scenarios do for the advanced rules what the base four attempted for the standard system: isolate a subsystem, then present a climax. They also require players to make or repurpose counters that were not supplied in DL11, including active-hero and interception markers and a Berem artefact counter. [S05]

This dependence complicates the claim that the article is a smooth optional layer. The player needs the magazine, blank counters, copied statistics, and careful reconciliation with rules it replaces. The expansion is inventive but physically external to the product. [S05]

## 30. Simplicity versus incompleteness

The central assessment cannot be reduced to “simple good” or “rules bad.” Three kinds of brevity coexist:

### Deliberate abstraction

- no economy or technology system;
- one political attempt per side;
- one leader modifier per battle;
- no base supply or command control;
- story events drawn from a staged cup;
- a short odds-and-modifiers combat procedure. [S01, S05]

### Productive compression

- political entry simultaneously changes force, geography, and objectives;
- two-step armies and national replacement pools model attrition without points;
- initiative controls whole-turn order with one roll;
- racial terrain and movement exceptions give forces identity;
- a small event set produces the war’s escalation and reversal. [S01]

### Under-specification and production error

- no general base rule for leader fate when a stack is destroyed;
- ten counters missing reverse-side statistics;
- poor explanation of counter colours;
- ambiguous action after a defender retreats before combat;
- a moving-capital rule that collides with a scenario objective;
- scenario-two setup instructions referring to absent or unexplained forces;
- a later reviewer’s report of more than twenty serious omissions after repeated play. [S05–S07]

The first two are design choices. The third is not depth reserved for experts; it is work transferred to players. A short rulebook can be easy to read and still fail to answer situations its own mechanisms create. [S06–S07]

## 31. Physical interface and information burden

The watercolour-style map and two-tone counters attracted contemporary praise. *Space Gamer/Fantasy Gamer* initially found the package unusually attractive for its low $10 price. The broad Ansalon map offered Dragonlance players a strong visual orientation object even outside play. [S03, S07, S09]

The physical weaknesses were equally concrete:

- the two map sheets were thin and vulnerable to tearing;
- counter backs were imperfectly aligned or missing information;
- no adequate colour key explained every national counter;
- stacks obscured information;
- charts and rules were distributed among rulebook, scenario booklet, cover, map, and later magazine article;
- approximately 400 counters plus national setup lists made setup and verification substantial. [S01, S05–S07]

The interface therefore offered **macro clarity** and **micro friction**. The whole war could be seen on one attractive surface, but operating the force roster demanded colour recognition, stacking, list checking, multiple dice, and occasional rules adjudication. [S06–S07]

## 32. Campaign integration: evocative output, weak feedback loop

The best-supported integration method is one-way:

1. play or advance the strategic game;
2. record evil-army locations by month;
3. use those locations to change local encounters, refugees, battles, rumours, and military presence in AD&D. [S01]

The board game can also be synchronised loosely to the module dates. The printed chart maps Battle Turn 20 to the cluster of early adventure events and later turns to DL9, DL12, DL13, and DL14. A referee may start the role-playing campaign after playing nineteen strategic turns or use the Historical Campaign. [S01]

The reverse direction is less complete. The prologue says character actions can affect the war and suggests inserting orbs and dragonlances when the modules discover them. It does not provide a general conversion table for:

- a player-character killing a Highlord;
- a city saved in an adventure;
- a failed quest;
- a local BATTLESYSTEM result;
- an alliance negotiated in role-play;
- a strategic army encountering the party. [S01, S09–S10]

The product can generate an alternate history very well at the map level. A dedicated referee must author much of the actual bidirectional coupling. *White Dwarf* recognised both the appeal and the bookkeeping burden, and also noted the awkward timing: by DL11, many groups following the module line had already begun their canonical campaigns. [S06]

## 33. Solo play means two-handed play

The prologue says the referee can play the game “solo or with a friend.” There is no programmed opponent, solitaire flowchart, priority table, hidden enemy plan, or faction-specific bot. Solo play means controlling both coalitions and attempting to make credible decisions for each. [S01]

The game otherwise assumes two opposing decision makers. More people can divide into teams, but the command structure remains Highlord versus Whitestone. Optional hidden stacks create uncertainty only when separate players maintain information; they do not create an autonomous enemy. [S01]

This distinction is important to the later SSI lineage. The computer game does not merely automate arithmetic and counter movement; it supplies a Highlord opponent so one person can occupy only the Whitestone role. That changes the psychological object from a self-refereed simulation to an adversarial solo game. [S15]

## 34. Balance and campaign volatility

No side win rates, tournament results, designer balance notes, or large after-action corpus were located. Balance can therefore be assessed only structurally and through sparse testimony. [S01, S09, S11]

Potential Highlord advantages include:

- first-turn initiative;
- an active core while Whitestone begins empty;
- dragons’ double combat leverage;
- draconian production;
- the ability to force political resolution by invasion;
- flying citadels and deep conquest threats;
- positive feedback when countries collapse. [S01]

Potential Whitestone counterweights include:

- national affinities among many good-aligned countries;
- invasion backlash in favour of the non-aggressor;
- threat-driven Knight activation;
- dragonlances and good dragons;
- the Golden General and other later events;
- time to create a broad coalition;
- the Highlord need to defend its temple and core while expanding. [S01]

The same systems that create the intended arc can destabilise it:

- several early activation failures delay the only Whitestone force growth;
- initiative can produce consecutive enemy operations;
- dragons and major modifiers widen combat variance;
- a lightly defended conquest site can erase a nation;
- event order can accelerate or delay the air-power reversal;
- advanced diplomacy can deactivate or flip a whole ally;
- advanced hero death can remove a high-leverage agent. [S01, S05]

The retrospective comments contain both a childhood favourite played intensively and a player who was crushed once and never returned. These are perfectly plausible outcomes under a volatile campaign, but two anecdotes cannot establish which was common. [S09]

## 35. Contemporary reception: the concept was stronger than the finish

Graham Staplehurst’s June 1986 *White Dwarf* review found the rules extremely easy for an experienced simulation player to learn, praised the appearance of the counters, and acknowledged the campaign-history idea. His overall judgement was adverse because the product was poorly organised, unclear in simple situations, physically uneven, insufficiently evocative during counter movement, and burdensome as a campaign aid. He recommended it only narrowly to experienced Dragonlance enthusiasts. [S06]

The most valuable parts of the review are its examples:

- the capital-disbanding rule appears to defeat *The Fall of Silvanesti* objective;
- the rules do not say whether an attacker advancing after an air/cavalry pre-combat retreat may attack another target. [S06]

Doug Traversa’s October–November 1987 review is still more damaging because it reports one basic scenario and two campaign games. He praised the map, counters, readable rules, low price, and familiar *Divine Right* foundation. He then reported more than twenty serious omissions and multiple setup errors. His reproduced example had players searching for Sancrist armies that did not exist and for Neraka forces whose identity as draconians was unexplained. He shelved the game pending comprehensive errata while explicitly saying that proper repair could make it enjoyable. [S07]

The distinction matters. Neither reviewer said there was no promising game. Both described an appealing object whose rules and production failed to support it reliably. The advanced article fixed missing reverse-side values on ten counters and added leader fate, but no comprehensive official answer to Traversa’s larger list was found. [S05–S07]

## 36. Evidence of sustained engagement

There is no representative retention evidence. The surviving record instead contains several small traces:

- a retrospective owner remembered the simple game fondly and remained inspired by its alternate-history/campaign-link concept;
- one commenter described intensive childhood play and a favourite DL product;
- another remembered a single crushing defeat and permanent abandonment;
- a 2020 VASSAL project digitised the base game and then experimented with population-based counter balance;
- a 2022 owner publicly prepared the physical game;
- a 2026 hobby developer began a rules-following standalone digital version;
- museum, archive, and current PDF records preserve the object;
- BoardGameGeek retains fewer than one hundred ratings in the observed search snapshot. [S08–S14]

These traces support a bounded conclusion: the game retained a small imaginative afterlife disproportionate to its polished completeness. They do not show a broad competitive community, regular organized play, strong commercial reprint history, a reliable completion rate, or common repeat play. [S09–S14]

The forms of afterlife are revealing. Fans repeatedly try to **operate, digitise, rebalance, or extend** the strategic substrate. That labour can indicate affection for the premise and map-scale relationships, but it may equally reflect dissatisfaction with the original implementation. Preservation effort is not independent validation of balance. [S12–S14]

## 37. Relationship to SSI’s *War of the Lance*

The relationship is too dense to be accidental and too transformative to call a literal port.

### Directly recognisable substrate

Both games contain:

- the continent-wide Highlord versus Whitestone war;
- a beginning in which Neraka is active and Whitestone must assemble;
- many politically neutral countries;
- territorial access controlled by allegiance;
- national armies, leaders, dragons, fleets, griffons, pegasi, and citadels;
- racial terrain and movement differences;
- initiative followed by whole-side movement and combat;
- reinforcement/replacement and national conquest;
- Highlord draconian forces;
- dragonlances, orbs, wizards, and story-linked strategic assets;
- Knight-country and Neraka-centred decisive objectives;
- an alternate campaign and a later historical start. [S01, S15]

### Computer-game reworking

SSI’s design adds or materially changes:

- a square map with eight-direction movement rather than DL11’s physical hex grid;
- named diplomats assigned to countries and an alignment/alliance model rather than one base activation roll;
- Highlord declarations of war;
- automated champion quests, wound, capture, rescue, and discovery states;
- subversion missions that remove field units temporarily;
- operation points and fatigue;
- quality growth and more detailed unit strength;
- reconnaissance and computer-managed information;
- a much longer phase sequence;
- setup controls for combat strength, replacements, alliance probability, and side advantage;
- automated resolution and battle display;
- a computer-controlled Highlord opponent. [S15]

The computer game’s heroes, artefacts, capture, and diplomacy also resemble subjects restored by the *Dragon* 107 advanced rules more than the thin character layer in the standard board game. SSI does not reproduce that article procedure by procedure. Its champions act mainly through automated quest reports, while its diplomats are allocated administratively rather than moved as active heroes. [S05, S15]

The safest classification is:

> *Dragons of Glory* is the direct tabletop **design precursor and strategic substrate** of SSI’s licensed *War of the Lance*; SSI’s title is a substantial computer adaptation and reworking, not a screen transcription.

The credit boundary supports that wording. SSI credits David Landrey with game design and programming, Chuck Kroegel with initial game design and graphics, and Bret Berry with development. Niles and Hickman are not credited as the computer designers. The SSI manual does not explicitly name DL11, and no development memorandum or designer interview was found that decomposes inherited and new work. [S15–S16]

## 38. What the computer medium repaired—and changed

The SSI version addresses several tabletop burdens:

- the computer knows which units exist and where they can set up;
- it tracks operation points, fatigue, strength, country status, diplomats, quests, and victory;
- it resolves combat and replacement procedure consistently;
- it moves hidden or opposing forces without requiring self-deception;
- it provides a one-player adversary;
- it can show messages when political and narrative states change. [S15]

It also creates new friction:

- a long administrative phase procession;
- limited viewport and map legibility;
- one-unit-at-a-time movement;
- machine waiting and disk access on period hardware;
- opaque formulas and automated quest outcomes;
- an opponent whose behaviour can be learned and exploited. [S15–S16]

The lineage is therefore not merely “board game made convenient.” The computer reallocates agency. It reduces physical adjudication, expands hidden state and bookkeeping, gives the player detailed diplomacy and army tempo, automates much of the heroic story, and fixes solo perspective to Whitestone against the Highlord AI. [S15]

## 39. The design’s strongest achievements

These are case-level findings, not recommendations for the future project.

### 39.1 A war arc without scripted history

Starting asymmetry, neutral activation, threat-driven mobilisation, staged events, dragon dominance, and later counter-assets can reproduce the broad emotional contour of the saga while allowing the map history to diverge. [S01]

### 39.2 Political state changes the playable map

An alliance is not an abstract score increment. It creates armies, access, objectives, ports, and vulnerabilities at once. An invasion can force the neutral to choose and may strengthen the opponent. [S01]

### 39.3 Fantasy mobility has strategic consequences

Dragons, pegasi, griffons, wizards, fleets, the Maelstrom, and flying citadels do not merely add combat colour. They change which fronts, islands, capitals, and rear fortresses are safe. [S01]

### 39.4 Attrition is compressed but meaningful

Two-step armies, national replacement pools, pairwise return, irreplaceable special units, draconian creation, and national conquest create force exhaustion without an economy sheet. [S01]

### 39.5 Scenario progression recognises novice needs

The product contains a genuine teaching ladder from a very small combat introduction to a complete short battle and then campaigns. Its recommended order is awkwardly communicated, but the underlying instructional intent is clear. [S01]

### 39.6 It proposed a playable alternate canon

The referee could generate a different War of the Lance rather than merely reenact the novels. Even a critic who found the integration vague recognised the idea’s imaginative power, and later fan projects repeatedly return to it. [S01, S09, S12–S13]

## 40. Principal fault lines

### 40.1 Political and event agency is thin in the standard game

One activation roll and one random event per turn carry enormous consequence. The player chooses targets and responds strategically, but does not build influence or conduct the saga’s quests. [S01]

### 40.2 Conquest can create disproportionate collapse

An airborne or amphibious coup against ungarrisoned sites can permanently remove a whole national army. This makes rear defence meaningful but can turn a local oversight into an irreversible campaign cascade. [S01]

### 40.3 Major modifiers can dominate ordinary force judgement

Dragons, lances, wizards, leaders, fortresses, and event devices produce large shifts on a d10 CRT. The fantasy layer is consequential, but event timing and single-roll variance can outweigh slower positional work. [S01]

### 40.4 The base character layer is mostly symbolic

Leaders are ratings; famous quest achievements are random events. The advanced game restores hero agency only by adding a second, more intricate rules centre. [S01, S05]

### 40.5 Advanced detail includes exploitable thresholds

One-army stacks forage while two-army stacks require supply; diplomacy can turn whole nations; command requires repeated national/racial checks. Realism and complexity rise together, but robustness does not rise uniformly. [S05]

### 40.6 The physical implementation is under-finished

Missing counter values, absent counter keys, unclear interaction rules, scenario setup mistakes, and no located comprehensive errata make some “simplicity” adjudication work for the players. [S05–S07]

### 40.7 Solo is not adversarial without another person

The referee can play both hands and generate history, but no original heuristic opponent exists. [S01]

## 41. Unknowns that should remain unknown

The available evidence does not establish:

- which side wins more often under standard or advanced rules;
- whether the full campaign commonly reaches Turn 30;
- how many players adopted the advanced article;
- the complete contents of Traversa’s missing rules-question list;
- whether TSR ever replied or issued further errata;
- how many copies were sold;
- how many role-playing groups actually used generated strategic history;
- typical setup, turn, and total play times beyond the designers’ six-to-eight-hour estimate;
- whether the 1985 catalogue date reflects manufacture, solicitation, or early release;
- the exact design-document pathway from DL11 to SSI;
- representative reasons for repeat play or abandonment. [S01–S16]

These gaps should not be filled with BoardGameGeek averages, isolated memories, or the existence of fan remakes.

## 42. Evidence-backed case findings

1. *Dragons of Glory* is a self-contained strategic board wargame placed unusually inside an AD&D module series; it does not require AD&D or BATTLESYSTEM to play. [S01–S03]
2. The best-supported release chronology is early 1986, despite persistent 1985 catalogue records. [S01–S03, S08]
3. TSR says prior Dragonlance readers requested a board game, making novice crossing a declared product aim. No outcome survey survives. [S04]
4. The base design models the whole War of the Lance with very little economy or administration: politics, geography, attrition, conquest, initiative, and staged story events carry most of the depth. [S01]
5. The full and Historical campaigns deliberately offer deep counterfactual history and late canonical divergence. [S01]
6. The four base scenarios form a genuine tutorial ladder, but the chronological printed order obscures the recommended learning order. [S01]
7. Highlord and Whitestone are temporally asymmetric: concentrated early aggressor versus initially inactive coalition with threat-driven and narrative late counters. [S01]
8. Neutral activation is simultaneously recruitment, route opening, objective creation, and political risk. [S01]
9. Initiative is a high-leverage order-control roll that can create consecutive operations across turns. [S01]
10. Fantasy forces change map topology and rear-area safety, not merely combat flavour. [S01]
11. Two-step armies and national replacement pools produce ablation without a resource economy; national conquest can turn a local coup into systemic collapse. [S01]
12. Standard leaders are mechanically thin, while strategic events abstract many character achievements into random arrivals. [S01]
13. The advanced article moves the design toward hero agency, diplomacy, artefact search, command, supply, interception, and seasonal geography. It is effectively an alternate higher-complexity mode. [S05]
14. Advanced rules add valuable operational texture but also high-leverage hero rolls, command checking, physical counter requirements, and a conspicuous supply threshold exploit. [S05]
15. The short base rules combine deliberate abstraction, productive compression, and genuine under-specification. Those categories must not be conflated. [S01, S05–S07]
16. Two contemporary reviewers liked aspects of the concept and presentation but documented concrete ambiguity and production failure; the later reviewer abandoned play after multiple attempts pending comprehensive errata. [S06–S07]
17. The campaign-aid concept is evocative but chiefly one-way. The board state can inform AD&D, while robust feedback from role-play into the strategic system is left to the referee. [S01, S06, S09–S10]
18. Original solo play is two-handed history generation, not play against a heuristic adversary. [S01]
19. A small but persistent afterlife exists through preservation, VASSAL, physical setup, reminiscence, and hobby digitisation. It is not representative retention evidence. [S08–S14]
20. Barry’s description of DL11 as the direct tabletop precursor to SSI’s *War of the Lance* is supported at the level of strategic substrate and product lineage. The computer game is nonetheless a substantial reworking with its own credited designers, expanded systems, automation, and AI. [S01, S05, S15–S16]

## 43. Deferred questions for the fireside discussion

No answer is assumed now, but this dossier makes several later prompts possible:

- Was the attraction the clean standard skeleton, the richer advanced ideas, or the imagined system they jointly implied?
- Did political activation feel tense, arbitrary, or both?
- Was the alternate-history campaign actually played, or was the map-scale possibility itself the memorable object?
- Did famous leaders and artefacts feel sufficiently present in the standard game?
- Were conquest cascades exciting operational opportunities or brittle “capital sniping”?
- Did the physical adjudication problems materially obstruct play, or were they repaired informally at the table?
- Was solo history generation ever part of the appeal?
- Which remembered features belong to DL11 and which were retrospectively fused with SSI’s *War of the Lance*?

Those are prompts for Barry’s unprompted recollection and later comparison. They are not design conclusions.
