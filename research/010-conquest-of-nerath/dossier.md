# Case 010: *Dungeons & Dragons: Conquest of Nerath*

**Design:** Richard Baker  
**Development and additional design:** Richard Baker, Peter Lee, Mons Johnson  
**Publisher:** Wizards of the Coast / Hasbro  
**Release:** 21 June 2011  
**Players:** 2–4; all four realms are fielded at every count  
**Advertised playing time:** 120–240 minutes  
**Form:** Tabletop fantasy territorial-conquest wargame with team and free-for-all modes, realm Event decks, hero expeditions, and adjustable victory conditions  
**Research status:** Independent dossier complete; Barry's “strong influence” disclosure quarantined; personal evaluation deferred  
**Comparative-design status:** Deliberately deferred

Source IDs refer to the separate [source ledger](sources.md). Barry's pre-discussion disclosure is preserved separately in [barry-context.md](barry-context.md).

## 1. Scope and evidence boundary

Unlike a computer game revised across patches, *Conquest of Nerath* is a comparatively clean research object: one English-language boxed release from 2011, no official expansion, and no revised edition located. Its official rules, eighty Event cards, thirty Treasures, thirty Dungeon Guardians, four fixed setups, and one map define the shipped design. Unofficial aids, variants, virtual-tabletop reproductions, and attempted digital ports belong to its afterlife, not to that object. [S01–S03, S17–S20]

Barry supplied two excellent local transcriptions. The first is a mechanics-focused rendering of the official rulebook; the second renders Universal Head's four-page v1.1 summary and player aid. The original PDFs linked in their headers were no longer present at those paths, so the official transcription was checked against a separately preserved English scan and the compact transcription against the public Order of Gamers copy. One presentational discrepancy was resolved: the surviving PDF has fourteen sheets because printed pages are paired and a Win2PDF notice was appended; the numbered game rulebook runs through page 24. “Thirteen PDF sheets of game material” and “a 24-numbered-page printed rulebook” are both more precise than simply calling it thirteen pages. [S01–S02]

Barry has disclosed that this is a strong influence on his vision. That statement affects neither the favourable nor adverse weighting below. It does not establish why the game mattered to him, which mechanisms he liked, how often he played, or what should survive into a future project. Those questions remain for the later fireside discussion.

## 2. Identity, credits, and recognition

The printed rulebook distinguishes original design from development more precisely than later catalogues. It credits Richard Baker with **Design**, then Richard Baker, Peter Lee, and Mons Johnson with **Development and Additional Design**. Jennifer Clarke Wilkes edited; Adam Gillespie illustrated the board; Wayne England illustrated tokens; William O'Connor painted the cover; and Keven Smith and Emi Tanji handled graphic design. BoardGameGeek and the Origins record conventionally list Baker, Johnson, and Lee together as designers, but that later convention should not erase the rulebook's division of labour. [S01, S07, S17]

The game released on 21 June 2011 at a US MSRP of $79.99. Its identifiers were SKU 338060000, UPC 653569609087, and ISBN 978-0-7869-5849-8. It won Best Board Game at the 38th Origins Awards ceremony in 2012, an award for 2011 releases. Calling this both a “2011 Origins Award” and a 2012 ceremony winner is common; the release itself remains 2011. [S06–S07]

Contemporary reviewers repeatedly positioned it near *Axis & Allies*, *Risk*, *Nexus Ops*, and the Milton Bradley Gamemaster line. That resemblance was not an accidental critical shortcut. Baker explicitly named *Risk*, *Axis & Allies*, *Empires of the Middle Ages*, *Greyhawk Wars*, *Divine Right*, and other light wargames among his foundations. [S04, S08–S14]

## 3. A seven-year design made to stage fantasy war

Baker received the assignment to create a D&D strategy wargame in spring 2005. The project was codenamed “Demogorgon.” He built demonstration kits and played the design heavily, but changed business plans put it on hold. Wizards revived it around 2010; Peter Lee, who had joined the company in 2008, began development in mid-2010 and recalled that the basic game was already strong enough that most changes were tweaks. Baker later called the seven years between design and publication a labour of love. [S04–S05]

The original map was not tied to an established D&D world. Baker invented much of its geography and names while borrowing famous dungeon names. During development the team recognised that it aligned well with the implied world around the Nentir Vale and the fallen Empire of Nerath, then revised the map, allegiances, and story accordingly. The result is an unusual setting object: it presents a world-scale political map for D&D's lightly specified fourth-edition “Points of Light” setting while retaining dungeons such as the Tomb of Horrors as evocative landmarks. [S01, S04]

The desired fantasy was not an adventuring party enlarged by arithmetic. Baker wanted the “cast of thousands” spectacle that tabletop D&D rarely delivered: footsoldier legions, cavalry-like Heroes, siege engines, fleets, giants, elementals, and dragons colliding in an epic war. Knights, krakens, and light fliers were considered and cut. Storm Elementals absorbed the sea-creature and light-flying roles; the mounted Fighter sculpt absorbed part of the knight fantasy. This pruning matters: the final roster aims for broad silhouettes and a few powerful exceptions, not an encyclopaedia of fantasy troop types. [S04]

## 4. The physical proposition

The box contains:

- one large territorial board;
- 252 plastic figures, 63 for each realm;
- 80 realm Event cards, 20 per realm;
- 30 Treasure cards and 30 Dungeon Guardian tokens;
- 16 polyhedral dice from d6 through d20;
- 80 control markers, 50 stacking chips, 2 team markers, and 80 Gold tokens;
- four realm reference cards, one Quick Reference card, a rulebook, and a fitted storage tray. [S01, S06]

Every realm has the same nine mechanical piece classes, but Footsoldiers, Fighters, Monsters, and Dragons receive realm-specific sculpts. Siege Engines, Wizards, Castles, Storm Elementals, and Warships share silhouettes across colours. The result combines instant mechanical recognition with visual faction flavour without multiplying unit rules. Several reviewers singled out the spectacle, board clarity, sculpt variety, labelled insert, and unusually easy pack-away as major attractions. The same abundance produced opposing criticism: a large table object, initial setup labour, fiddly Gold and markers, thin card stock in one account, and too few supplied dice for the largest battles. [S01, S09–S14]

It is important not to mistake theme density for role-playing depth. The box has no characters with individual histories, levels, equipment slots, dialogue, or campaign persistence. Its “Fighter” and “Wizard” pieces are anonymous Hero classes; its dungeons have no maps or choices within them. D&D identity comes through dice, monsters, card names, art, realm fiction, and the structural connection between adventuring and war. [S01–S03]

## 5. Four realms, two default sides, or one free-for-all

The four realms are:

| Realm | Colour | Official position and flavour summary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dark Empire of Karkoth | Black | Aggressive land-war opening, large starting domain, first turn, weakest Event deck |
| Vailin Alliance | Gold | Elven and mercantile coalition with strong naval orientation |
| Iron Circle | Red | Goblin, mercenary, and infernal power described as a balanced military force with devious events |
| Nerathan League | Blue | Human and dwarven remnant, exposed underdog, last turn, strongest Event deck |

The primary mode is a “sides” game: Karkoth and the Iron Circle form Evil; Vailin and Nerath form Good. Baker's explanation is unusually explicit. In an open conquest game, some players dislike having to select a victim and become the socially culpable aggressor. A default war makes the attack part of a shared story rather than an arbitrary personal choice. Free-for-all remains supported, with informal agreements that are never binding, but fixed alliances supply the game's most natural script. [S01, S04]

This is design of social permission as much as military balance. “Karkoth attacks Nerath” is what the fiction says should happen. The narrative lowers the interpersonal cost of attacking and makes immediately hostile borders legible. It also removes formal diplomacy from the primary game. Allies cannot betray, trade, merge economies, transport each other's land pieces, or combine attacks on one active turn. They can share friendly spaces, defend together, and win through shared totals. [S01, S04]

All four realms must be fielded:

| Human players | Control arrangement |
| ---: | --- |
| 2 | One controls Karkoth and Iron Circle; the other controls Vailin and Nerath |
| 3 | One controls both Good realms; the other two separately control the allied Evil realms |
| 4 alliance | One realm each in the fixed 2-v-2 sides |
| 4 free-for-all | One realm each, nonbinding agreements, everyone attackable |

This keeps the strategic geography intact but makes player count a substantial experiential variable. Two players each alternate between two treasuries, hands, and turns. Three-player play gives one person twice the realm administration while the other two collaborate. Four-player alliance play gives every realm a human advocate and was the most consistently preferred form in contemporary reviews. [S01, S09–S11]

## 6. A fixed opening built to start in contact

The map, starting ownership, armies, incomes, dungeons, and realm order are fixed. Enemy enclaves sit inside or near each realm's natural sphere. Peter Lee described those as deliberate “immediate conflicts”: inviting targets give each player an obvious opening problem and generate forward momentum before anyone can settle into peaceful accumulation. Starting silhouettes are printed on the board and full setups appear on the realm references, reducing a potentially formidable placement task to matching pieces to marked spaces. [S01, S04, S09, S11]

The fixed order is:

1. Karkoth
2. Vailin
3. Iron Circle
4. Nerath

Starting treasury and base income partially compensate later positions:

| Realm | Starting treasury | Base income before territorial changes |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Karkoth | 10 | 15 |
| Vailin | 11 | 13 |
| Iron Circle | 12 | 13 |
| Nerath | 13 | 14 |

Each realm also begins with two cards from its unique Event deck, draws a third at the beginning of its first turn, and thereafter draws one per turn. The design article acknowledges the core problem without euphemism: fixed positions can become deterministic once optimal openings are found. Event cards were added as a “strong dose” of randomness and later divided into realm decks to create identity and compensation. [S01, S04]

That solution does not eliminate scripted openings. Cards can redirect or magnify an opening, and a short game will expose only part of a twenty-card deck, but the board position and order remain known. The design deliberately exchanges the generative uncertainty of variable setup for immediate conflict, thematic asymmetry, and a lower first-turn question burden. Positive reviewers found the game different enough through cards, purchases, and Treasures; adverse reviewers saw repeated first moves and a limited tactical ceiling. Both observations can be true at different levels of familiarity. [S04, S09, S11, S15–S16]

## 7. The six-step turn

Every realm takes a complete turn through the same sequence:

1. **Draw:** take one realm Event card.
2. **Move:** move any or all pieces and declare every battle by entering hostile spaces or dungeon entrances.
3. **Fight Battles:** resolve all triggered sea battles first, then land and dungeon battles; score conquest VP.
4. **Reposition:** move flying pieces a second time and return Heroes from dungeons.
5. **Reinforce:** buy pieces, place them at Castles, and move eligible new pieces through friendly spaces.
6. **Collect Income:** receive Gold for controlled land spaces.

Draw and Collect Income are mandatory when possible; the other steps are voluntary. The whole realm activates before the next player. This produces an easily recited command grammar and lets the active player make coherent offensives across the map. It also concentrates thought and handling in one player's turn while others wait. [S01–S02]

Several ordering details create more structure than the six verbs imply:

- all movement decisions precede any battle result;
- the attacker chooses the order of battles, subject to all sea battles preceding land battles;
- no ordinary reinforcement can join once fighting starts;
- flying units can attack and then reposition, or reposition from one friendly space to another after not fighting;
- purchases occur after combat, but newly placed mobile pieces may immediately move through friendly territory during reinforcement, not fight;
- income arrives at turn end, so conquest expands the resources available on the next turn rather than paying for the current offensive. [S01–S02]

This is low verb count, not low interaction count. A player can learn the top-level turn quickly while still consulting rules for transport, sea landings, retreats, dungeons, Castle capture, allied casualties, and card timing.

## 8. Movement, geography, and logistics

Land pieces usually move one or two adjacent spaces; Dragons move three. Entering an enemy-controlled space, even an empty one, ends movement and schedules a battle. Castles do not move. Flying pieces can cross land and sea, pass over hostile spaces without stopping, fight in either environment, and move again during Reposition. Storm Elementals can end the turn safely at sea; Dragons combine range, a d20, and durability. [S01–S02]

Warships create the most intricate physical subsystem. A ship moves up to two sea spaces and carries up to two land pieces. It can load along its route, bridge between coasts without moving, unload into one or two adjacent coastal spaces, or launch a sea landing. Non-Hero cargo does not fight at sea. If its Warship is destroyed, all cargo dies. Embarked Fighters and Wizards can fight in a sea battle; if their ship survives, they may disembark and fight again in a land battle or enter a dungeon. Allied pieces cannot be transported. [S01–S02]

An amphibious offensive can therefore be a dependency chain:

`load before land movement → survive interception at sea → preserve carrying ships → debark → combine with overland attackers → win or find a valid retreat`

This gives fleets real strategic meaning rather than making sea spaces decorative. Ships transport force, establish bridges, threaten coasts, protect or expose Heroes, and determine retreat routes. It also produces a disproportionate share of rule exceptions. A seemingly simple territorial map has embarked state, cargo restrictions, sea-first battle order, double participation for Heroes, and conditional unloading. [S01, S08, S10–S12]

The Reposition step makes air power different again. A flying force can concentrate for battle and disperse afterward, move through hostile lines, or cover large distances without a transport chain. That mobility becomes especially consequential for Dragons because one hit is absorbed and healed after battle. Baker's later stack restriction addresses their mutual protection, not their individual fantasy or reach. [S01, S05]

## 9. Combat: one target number, several dice

The central resolution rule is exceptionally compact:

> Every attack die hits on 6 or higher; stronger pieces roll larger dice.

An ordinary round proceeds through First Strike, all other attacks, simultaneous ordinary damage, and the attacker's decision to press or retreat. The attacker must endure at least one round. Except for First Strike casualties, both sides receive their attacks before ordinary losses are removed. The owner of the target force decides which piece takes each hit. If both forces annihilate each other, the defender retains control. [S01–S02]

The die changes probability without changing the number players scan for:

| Die | Successful faces | Hit probability | Expected hits per die |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| d6 | 1 | 16.7% | 0.167 |
| d8 | 3 | 37.5% | 0.375 |
| d10 | 5 | 50.0% | 0.500 |
| d12 | 7 | 58.3% | 0.583 |
| d20 | 15 | 75.0% | 0.750 |

Shape, colour, printed die icon, and often the polygonal figure base all reinforce the mapping. Once the pieces are learned, a mixed army can gather its different dice and resolve its ordinary fire in one handful: look only for results of six or more. Contemporary reviewers repeatedly praised this as both tactile D&D theatre and lower arithmetic than resolving each unit class through a separate table. [S01, S04, S09–S12]

The simplicity is carefully bounded. First Strike kills before ordinary response. Siege Engines roll twice when attacking. Storm Elementals roll twice at sea. Dragons require two hits and heal a single hit after battle. Castles are not destroyed by damage; the first hit inactivates them until battle end. Cards can add dice, direct hits, rerolls, thresholds, pieces, or cancellations. Dungeon Guardians can require 7+ or 8+ and can have multiple dice functioning simultaneously as attacks and hit points. The constant threshold supplies a stable chassis, not a rule with no exceptions. [S01–S03]

| Piece | Cost | Attack | Move | Principal rule |

| Footsoldier | 1 | d6 | 1 | Cheap body; no special ability |
| Siege Engine | 2 | d8 | 1 | Rolls 2d8 when attacking |
| Fighter | 2 | d10 | 2 | Hero; explores dungeons and fights at sea while embarked |
| Wizard | 3 | d10 | 2 | Hero; First Strike; explores dungeons and fights at sea |
| Monster | 3 | d12 | 2 | Run Amok after a victorious land battle |
| Castle | 4 | d20 | — | Produces pieces, can be captured, cannot be destroyed normally |
| Storm Elemental | 3 | d8 | 2 | Flying; 2d8 at sea; may remain over water |
| Dragon | 5 | d20 | 3 | Flying; takes two hits; heals after battle |

Raw expected hits per Gold are informative but incomplete:

| Piece and context | Expected ordinary hits | Expected hits per Gold |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Footsoldier | 0.167 | 0.167 |
| Siege Engine defending | 0.375 | 0.188 |
| Siege Engine attacking | 0.750 | 0.375 |
| Fighter | 0.500 | 0.250 |
| Wizard before valuing First Strike | 0.500 | 0.167 |
| Monster before valuing Run Amok | 0.583 | 0.194 |
| Storm Elemental on land | 0.375 | 0.125 |
| Storm Elemental at sea | 0.750 | 0.250 |
| Dragon before valuing durability/mobility | 0.750 | 0.150 |
| Warship before valuing transport | 0.375 | 0.188 |

These figures explain why cost cannot be read as attack strength. Siege Engines are exceptionally efficient attackers but slow and much weaker on defence. Fighters buy movement, dungeon access, and sea participation as well as combat. Wizards pay for removing enemy attacks through First Strike. Monsters can convert a victory into one additional empty-territory conquest. Elementals and Warships have environmental roles. Dragons buy reach and a regenerating extra hit. Footsoldiers let an owner preserve all of those abilities by assigning casualties to cheap bodies. [S01–S04]

The table also falsifies one later essay's literal claim that Fighters are “uniformly” statistically superior at equal cost to Siege Engines and Monsters. Fighters are more versatile and may become stronger through Treasures, but an attacking Siege Engine produces more baseline expected hits per Gold. The criticism is still useful as evidence that experienced play can converge on Hero-heavy forces; its stated proof is not. [S16]

## 11. Casualty choice makes an army more than its total firepower

Because the owner assigns hits, an army is a protected package of capabilities. Footsoldiers do little damage but preserve Wizards, Siege Engines, Monsters, ships, and Dragons. A Dragon can absorb one hit for free if it survives the battle, but a second hit destroys five Gold and extreme mobility. Sacrificing a Hero can preserve the territorial army but forgo dungeon access. Losing a carrying Warship also loses cargo, so its apparent two-Gold value can understate the threatened investment. [S01–S02]

This creates a three-part purchase problem:

1. **How many attacks and special effects can the force generate?**
2. **How quickly and through which terrain can those capabilities arrive?**
3. **How many cheap losses can it sustain before valuable functions disappear?**

The attacker controls whether to continue but not which enemy capabilities a hit removes. The defender can sacrifice Footsoldiers while keeping First Strike, durability, or production alive. Conversely, a First Strike force can remove some bodies before they roll, changing the ordinary round. A battle is still a dice contest, but composition, target resilience, retreat space, and future use matter before the dice are lifted. [S01, S09–S12]

## 12. Economy, Castles, and territorial conversion

Every controlled land space generates one Gold during that realm's Collect Income step. The printed base income corresponds to starting territory; gained or lost starting spaces change the total. Gold remains in the treasury until spent. It buys any available piece class during Reinforce. There is no separate food, mana, production point, population, upkeep, or technology resource. [S01–S02]

Pieces are placed at Castles. A capital Castle has unlimited placement; another Castle normally receives no more than four pieces in a turn. If only one Castle remains under the realm's control, the ordinary cap is waived. Newly built or captured Castles generally cannot produce until a later turn. After placement, a new mobile piece can move through friendly spaces up to its allowance, making production less remote from the front than its board position suggests; it still cannot initiate combat that turn. [S01–S02]

Castles connect three systems:

- a defensive d20 that can be silenced but not killed;
- a reinforcement point whose capture changes future deployment;
- a territorial object that transfers intact to the attacker.

A captured capital is worth five VP, halves its former owner's income rounded up, and gives the occupier a Castle for later production. A captured capital itself produces only one Gold like another space, but its real effect is positional and punitive. Territory already supplies income; a Castle converts territorial reach into production reach; a capital capture simultaneously adds score and weakens an economy. [S01]

This is a positive-feedback conquest economy. Success can increase future Gold, reduce an enemy's Gold, move reinforcement closer, preserve a captured production node, and add irreversible VP. The variable victory threshold was designed to stop that feedback before it requires laborious total elimination, not to remove the feedback itself. [S01, S04]

## 13. Soft faction asymmetry through decks and position

The realms do not have unique unit statistics, costs, movement, or technology trees. Their mechanical identity comes from three other layers:

1. **position and time:** fixed geography, starting forces, enemy enclaves, treasury, base income, and turn slot;
2. **realm Event deck:** twenty unique cards with different timing, effects, and thematic concentration;
3. **emergent Treasure portfolio:** random persistent and one-shot powers that can redirect later purchases.

The direct development account says Karkoth's deck is intentionally weakest because Karkoth moves first and has the largest starting territory; Vailin is stronger and naval/ranged; Iron Circle is also naval but devious; Nerath is strongest to compensate for moving last and beginning under pressure. A typical short or medium game reveals only a portion of each deck, so players know the realm's possibility space without knowing which answers are in hand. [S03–S04]

The complete card transcriptions make the thematic tendencies concrete:

| Realm | Recurring Event tendencies, not an exhaustive rule identity |
| --- | --- |
| Karkoth | Raising and transforming undead, attrition, direct land pressure, plunder, Monsters, and destructive dragon effects |
| Vailin | Fleets, winds, blockades, privateers, naval creation and mobility, nature, archery, and broad rerolls |
| Iron Circle | Siege and fire, assassination and treachery, conscription, direct damage, transfers, and tactical surprise |
| Nerath | Militia, defence, healing and hit cancellation, uprisings, Heroes, and effects that become stronger as home territory is lost |

This is “soft” asymmetry because a Vailin Footsoldier remains statistically identical to a Karkoth Footsoldier. It avoids requiring four unit encyclopaedias, while position and cards push the same pieces toward different stories. The cost is that faction identity can be hostage to card draw. A Vailin hand saturated with naval effects may be vivid on the right front and unhelpful elsewhere; a Nerath catch-up card is powerful only when its condition arises. [S01, S03–S04, S11]

## 14. Heroes, dungeons, guardians, and Treasures

Fighters and Wizards are Heroes. They can remain with armies, fight at sea while embarked, or leave the territorial front to enter dungeon entrances. At a dungeon, face-down Guardians are revealed and an opponent rolls them. Guardians use the ordinary battle sequence but can have multiple attack dice, First Strike, rewards, or defence thresholds requiring Heroes to roll 7+ or 8+. Their dice are also their hit capacity: each hit removes one die, and a surviving Guardian regenerates to full if the Heroes retreat or fail. [S01–S03]

Winning yields one secret Treasure card regardless of the number of surviving Heroes. The dungeon is immediately restocked with two face-down Guardians, making repeated visits harder in token count but not permanently exhausted. During Reposition the Heroes return to an adjacent friendly space. This is not spatial exploration inside a dungeon; it is a compact expedition battle inserted into the world map. [S01–S02]

Treasures do three jobs at once:

- score one to three VP when played;
- deliver a one-shot **Power**, a persistent **Property**, or both;
- alter the value of later pieces and routes.

The complete local transcription contains 30 cards: 17 worth one VP, 12 worth two, and one worth three. Twenty-eight have persistent Properties; four have one-shot Powers; three have both. Effects include First Strike for a class, rerolls, double hits at high results, global unit movement, Castle-to-Castle transfer, extra income, free reinforcements, Hero flight, rescue from destruction, direct damage, and reconnaissance. Most rewards therefore do not merely cash out a dungeon for score: they continue changing the war. [S03]

The Hero decision is a real allocation trade rather than a separate solitaire bonus. A Fighter assigned to a dungeon is not defending a border or joining an offensive. A Wizard in a dungeon risks an expensive First Strike piece. More Heroes improve expedition survival but reduce the field army. Treasure then feeds back into the territorial game, can strengthen a unit class, and can itself satisfy a victory condition. [S01, S04, S08–S12]

This makes the design two linked games rather than two independent minigames:

`territorial income and safety → spare Heroes for dungeons → Treasure score and powers → changed army efficiency and mobility → territorial pressure`

The loop is one of the most frequently praised features. It is also a plausible amplifier: the player who can already spare Heroes may collect persistent advantages and repeatedly farm nearby entrances, while the player under invasion cannot divert resources. The second Guardian token increases risk but does not guarantee catch-up. [S09–S16]

## 15. Victory: one map, several clocks

Before play, the group chooses short, medium, or long conditions. Normal and alliance thresholds differ:

| Mode | Short | Medium | Long |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Normal / free-for-all | 13 VP, most at round end | 20 VP, most at round end | Hold own and all enemy capitals at turn end, or have at least 8 Treasures and the most at round end |
| Alliance | 20 shared VP, most at round end | 30 shared VP, most at round end | Allies control all capitals or collectively hold 12 Treasures |

In short and medium games, a player scores immediately but cannot win until every realm has completed the round. Reaching the threshold early does not deny later players a final turn. Ties extend the game. Long-game capital victory is checked at the end of the active player's turn and is immediate; the Treasure route is checked at round end. [S01–S02]

Victory points record actions:

- 1 VP for conquering an enemy land space;
- 5 VP for conquering an enemy capital;
- 1–3 VP for playing a Treasure.

Retaking one's own starting territory scores nothing. If an enemy later retakes and loses one of its spaces again, the attacker can score that conquest again. Score therefore remembers successful incursions rather than representing the present map. An empire can be smaller now but retain points from earlier attacks; a realm can liberate its land without erasing the invader's score. [S01]

This decoupling is deliberate. Baker wanted to avoid the familiar end state in which one player is clearly ahead but everyone must continue until the last territory is erased. A short-game lead is allowed to stand in for eventual victory; players who want more visible domination can select a longer condition. The abandoned dragon-bounty rule shows the same concern for behaviour: rewarding dragon kills made owners excessively cautious and created adjudication problems, so it was removed. [S04]

The clocks are not simply three durations of one strategic problem. Short play increases the value of first-round conquests and a lucky early Treasure. Medium gives card, purchase, retaliation, and dungeon engines more time. Long abandons ordinary VP and turns capitals or Treasure count into the objective, changing the meaning of territory, defence, and expedition access. Later balance accounts that disagree across lengths may therefore be observing genuinely different games, not merely noisy estimates of one balance point. [S15–S16]

## 16. What information the player sees

The strategic board is largely public. Territory control, pieces, Castles, Gold tokens, completed Treasures, dungeons already revealed, and VP are visible. There is no fog of war, hidden movement, or secret unit strength. Uncertainty is concentrated in four places:

- each realm's Event hand;
- unplayed Treasures and their concealed VP values;
- face-down Dungeon Guardians;
- dice outcomes. [S01–S03]

This division supports legible planning without perfect calculation. A player can count a fleet and trace its range, yet not know whether a card will create ships, cancel hits, transfer units, assassinate a Hero, or open a surprise territory. An opponent holding three Treasures may be concealing three VP or substantially more, and the cards may reconfigure a unit class when revealed. The board says what is possible in the ordinary rules; hands say why an apparent battle may not resolve ordinarily. [S01, S03–S04]

Hidden Treasures were a deliberate development change. Their values widened after they became secret so that an unrevealed hand would obscure the exact finishing threat. This adds tension and reduces perfect leader calculation, but it does not eliminate leader information: territorial score, income, army size, and number of hidden cards remain visible or inferable. In free-for-all play it can make coalition and kingmaking decisions less exact; in alliance play it creates a shared surprise reserve. [S04, S09]

Cards also produce timing load. Some are immediate, some are held indefinitely, and others trigger before a roll, after a victory, during Draw, at the start of a battle, at the end of a round, or in another player's step. There is no hand limit. Basic map information is open, but the complete tactical possibility set requires knowing up to eighty Event cards and thirty Treasures or accepting surprise. [S01, S03]

## 17. Teaching, reference, and table interface

The game uses several mutually reinforcing memory aids:

- a fixed six-step turn printed in summary form;
- starting silhouettes on the board and setup diagrams on realm cards;
- one constant ordinary hit threshold;
- die icons expressed by number, colour, and shape;
- polygonal bases that often match the required die;
- nine mechanically shared unit classes across all realms;
- a printed piece and victory summary;
- a compartmented tray labelled by component type. [S01–S02, S04]

This is why reviewers could describe a box with hundreds of pieces as quick to learn. The top-level question at each step is simple, the board begins with obvious pressure points, and players do not learn a different statistical roster for every colour. Richeson's group reported comparatively few rules questions and roughly ten-to-fifteen-minute setup once familiar. The Dread Gazebo reviewer said that by a third session the reference card was sufficient. Opinionated Gamers called the rules well written and accessible even to newcomers to the genre. [S09, S11, S13]

Accessibility should not be confused with a tiny rule set. The game has detailed cases for loading before movement, bridging, Heroes fighting twice in amphibious operations, sea-first resolution, flying retreat, allied casualty disagreement, Castle inactivation, capital income, new-piece movement, dungeon thresholds, Guardian regeneration, Event timing, and three different victory clocks. Most can be learned only when encountered; they are organised exceptions around a stable grammar. [S01–S02]

The afterlife of player aids exposes small interface gaps. The box has only one shared Quick Reference card. A fan redesigned all four realm references because cards placed on the designated areas could obstruct useful information and because the complete unit table was not repeated for every player. Other high-rated files condense piece data, teach the game, incorporate forum clarifications, or translate cards. These are signs of an approachable but reference-dependent design, not proof that the original interface failed. [S17]

Physical clarity has costs a computer interface would normally absorb: sorting 252 pieces, counting chips, maintaining Gold, selecting the right dice, tracking face-up/down Guardians, fitting stacks into territories, and remembering which cards are persistent. Several reviewers loved exactly that tactile abundance; others called it fiddly. The same component is theatre for one group and administration for another. [S09–S14]

## 18. What favourable reviewers most valued

No single review establishes “the best feature,” but positive evidence clusters consistently.

### 18.1 Immediate aggression without elimination cleanup

Enemy enclaves, close borders, new-piece movement, action-based VP, and adjustable thresholds give players reasons to attack from the opening round. The game does not require global extermination after the strategic result is apparent. Reviewers who liked this described it as fast, furious, offensive, or unusually easy to fit into an evening relative to a full conquest game. [S04, S08–S14]

### 18.2 A compact combat ritual with physical identity

The “6+ on different dice” rule makes unit strength visible and enjoyable to handle. Large mixed forces can roll together, while d6, d8, d10, d12, and d20 retain D&D's material vocabulary. Special abilities then make unit choice matter without requiring a combat-results table. [S04, S09–S13]

### 18.3 Armies that contain roles rather than one strength value

Cheap bodies, attacking Siege Engines, First Strike Wizards, Run Amok Monsters, flying naval specialists, transport fleets, durable Dragons, and productive Castles create recognisable functions. Casualty choice gives composition and protection meaning. Purchase decisions can favour land pressure, naval reach, Hero expeditions, high-value mobility, or attritional depth. [S01, S09–S12]

### 18.4 D&D adventure joined to grand war

Heroes are useful in armies but uniquely able to explore dungeons. Guardians use the combat grammar rather than a new game, and Treasures return score and persistent powers to the world conflict. Reviewers repeatedly treated this connection—not the mere presence of fantasy names—as the design's distinctive addition to the conquest form. [S08–S14]

### 18.5 Soft faction identity without four rules encyclopaedias

Shared piece mechanics lower learning cost; realm positions, turn slots, sculpts, and Event decks supply theme. Cards create undead resurgence, naval weather, infernal betrayal, and human resistance without permanent faction-specific exceptions on every figure. [S03–S04, S09–S13]

### 18.6 Adjustable commitment

Short, medium, and long conditions let a group choose an approximate commitment and a preferred meaning of victory. Positive reviewers found that point victory preserved enjoyment rather than feeling like an arbitrary truncation. Some explicitly praised a conquest game that could end before total military victory. [S04, S09–S12]

### 18.7 A generous, legible physical product

Board art, realm sculpts, polyhedral dice, card art, abundant pieces, rule examples, board setup markings, and the fitted insert made the game feel like an event. The $79.99 price was substantial, but several reviewers considered the contents commensurate; others regarded it as a barrier. [S01, S06, S09–S14]

The common favourable description is not “deep simulation.” It is a game whose primary actions are quickly grasped, whose board becomes violent immediately, and whose fantasy systems add colour and strategic detours without dissolving the conquest chassis.

## 19. Principal frictions and adverse evidence

The adverse record is not one counter-verdict. It identifies different ways the same systems can become costly.

1. **Fixed setup can become a script.** The designer himself anticipated solved opening moves. Random hands perturb the script without replacing the known order, positions, and targets. [S04, S09, S11]
2. **Sequential full turns create downtime.** A realm may move across many fronts, resolve several battles, buy pieces, and count income while three others wait. Armchair General particularly noted Iron Circle and Nerath inactivity during early turns; a 2012 fan variant rebuilt activation specifically to reduce waiting and analysis paralysis. [S10, S17]
3. **Four realms are compulsory.** Two players double-hand; three players create an asymmetric 2-realms-versus-one-realm-each social structure. The most natural player count is less flexible than the “2–4” label suggests. [S01, S09–S11]
4. **Luck is broad, not local.** Dice, Event draws, Guardian draws, Treasure draws, and hidden card timing all alter outcomes. A tactically sound plan can meet a direct-hit card, a poor battle roll, or an unusually valuable Treasure. [S01, S03, S10–S11]
5. **The victory score can reward leaving home unrecovered.** Liberating one's own territory gives no VP; invading another weak enemy space does. Persistent action score can make an indirect offensive rational when fiction suggests counterattack. [S01, S11, S15]
6. **Conquest rewards can compound.** A victory can add VP and income, capture a production point, remove enemy income, and open another front. A capital amplifies all of these. [S01, S15–S16]
7. **Treasure can amplify capacity rather than compensate weakness.** A realm with spare Heroes obtains both VP and mostly persistent Properties, becoming better able to afford and exploit another expedition. [S03, S15–S16]
8. **The dungeon fiction is mechanically thin.** There is no map, navigation, choice of room, narrative branch, or persistent Hero. Reveal tokens, fight, draw one card, restock: elegant integration can also feel like window dressing. [S01, S14]
9. **Realm asymmetry may be too soft or too draw-dependent.** Some reviewers wanted unique faction units; others found Vailin's naval concentration situational or weaker than advertised. [S11–S12]
10. **One unit balance problem is directly confirmed.** Baker concluded after release that groups of four or five Dragons were too good and recommended a stacking restriction. [S05, S13]
11. **Long play can outlast its decision variety.** Reviews report anything from about two hours to five or more, with long games especially likely to wear thin. A fixed map and narrow high-level loop may not provide enough novelty for every group over that duration. [S08–S11]
12. **The physical spectacle is labour.** Initial setup, sorting, stacks, Gold, cards, control markers, guardian tokens, and insufficient dice can slow handling. [S09–S14]
13. **There is no campaign, solo opponent, or official digital support.** Repeated play begins again from the same world state. Solitaire means controlling all sides; fan campaign and virtual-tabletop work appeared later. [S01, S17–S20]

These frictions interact. Static openings make turn order more consequential; full-turn activation makes a first-move advantage harder to interrupt; conquest converts tempo into income; persistent score does not recede when territory is liberated; and dungeons give the player with spare capacity a second compounding route.

## 20. Balance is disputed—and the disputes are not commensurate

The public record cannot support a clean declaration that Good, Evil, Karkoth, or any one mode is balanced or broken. The major accounts used different player counts, clocks, expertise, and even incorrectly labelled conditions:

| Evidence | Play condition | Observed conclusion | Evidential limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Designer/developer article [S04] | Development across positions | Turn order, income, position, and deck strength deliberately compensate one another | Intention and internal testing, not released win-rate evidence |
| GMS review [S12] | Alliance and free-for-all, details unspecified | Both felt surprisingly balanced | One strongly favourable reviewer; no game count or results |
| RPGnet [S11] | Three-player, novice group | Karkoth appeared very strong; deck/Gold compensation seemed insufficient | Mode-specific; reviewer declines a general imbalance claim |
| Boardgaming Way [S15] | Repeated four-player alliance, called “medium” at 20 VP | Evil and especially first-moving Karkoth/Iron Circle dominate; leader snowballs | Official medium alliance is 30 VP; 20 is short, so the tested clock is misclassified |
| Wilderness of Tygers [S16] | Three solo multi-hand trials | Opposite outcome: Good wins long; hypothesis that short favours Evil, long favours Good, medium is closest | Tiny solo sample; coordination and human differences absent |
| Baker retrospective [S05] | Post-release public experience | Dragon stacking is too strong | Strong confirmation of a unit issue, not faction or mode balance |

The Boardgaming Way source mismatch is particularly consequential. Its “medium 20 VP” games ended at the official short threshold. If Evil's advantage is early first-turn tempo and Good's compensation arrives through stronger cards and dungeon access, shortening the runway would produce exactly the reported result. This does not make the experience unreal; it means the article cannot establish that official medium play has the same failure. [S01, S15–S16]

The opposing solo account offers a plausible clock hypothesis, not a correction backed by enough trials. Three multi-hand games can reveal interactions but not estimate balance. It may also optimise all four sides through one mind and remove alliance communication costs. The responsible finding is therefore conditional:

- the fixed order creates a real and visible tempo asymmetry;
- the decks and position were explicitly built as compensation;
- at least one novice three-player group and one repeated short-threshold alliance group found that compensation insufficient;
- one solo analyst found it overcompensated in the long game;
- no controlled dataset resolves faction, alliance, or clock balance. [S04, S11–S16]

The Dragon issue is different. It appears in independent review and was conceded by the original designer. Baker's remedy allows no more than one Dragon of the same colour to end in a space; if several do, he says they fight until only one remains. It preserves individual mobility and durability while preventing a stack from sharing cheap regenerating hits. This is the clearest demonstrated balance defect, but the remedy remained a recommended house rule rather than a new edition. [S05, S13]

## 21. Anti-turtling and snowballing are the same structure viewed at two times

The design article openly identifies the runaway-leader problem in “build, conquer, gain resources, build more” games. *Conquest of Nerath* addresses the tedious end, not every cause. It lets a moderate lead end a short game before the leader must erase all resistance. That can be a major improvement in experienced time: the game stops when advantage is sufficient rather than asking losers to administer their defeat. [S04, S09, S12]

Its scoring and economy also manufacture forward motion:

- only enemy conquest and Treasures score;
- retaking home does not score;
- repeated future conquest of the same enemy land can score again;
- income follows control;
- captured Castles extend production;
- new units can move through friendly territory immediately;
- capital loss halves income;
- most Treasure effects persist. [S01–S03]

These rules make passivity costly. They also allow early success to persist in both score and productive capacity. A player can lose the territory just captured yet keep its VP; if still holding it at income collection, the player also gained Gold. A realm that liberates home repairs income but does not repair the score. A dungeon Treasure is both progress toward the threshold and an engine for later progress. [S01, S03]

Thus “anti-turtling” and “snowball” are not separate modules. Both arise because the game rewards action quickly and remembers it. The short threshold turns that acceleration into a bounded race; it does not create catch-up. Stronger late-player cards, hidden information, round-end victory checks, defensive Events, and free-for-all coalition are compensating forces, but their effectiveness varies by mode and draw. [S01, S03–S04, S11, S15–S16]

The persistent score also changes the fiction of territory. VP is not a census of the present empire. It is political memory of damage inflicted, strategic momentum, prestige, or an abstract case for negotiated victory. Reviewers divided on whether that abstraction was an elegant end to a world-conquest game or an incentive to raid weak borders while one's homeland burned. [S09, S12, S15]

## 22. Replayability and evidence of sustained engagement

The box offers several sources of variation despite one setup:

- four realm positions and two broad mode structures;
- different opening Event hands and only partial exposure of each twenty-card deck;
- random Guardian distribution and escalating dungeon restocks;
- hidden Treasures that can favour different unit purchases;
- short, medium, and long victory conditions;
- purchase, route, casualty, expedition, and alliance choices. [S01–S04]

This supports replay before it guarantees long retention. A new Treasure can make Fighters, Monsters, Wizards, Warships, Castles, or Dragons more attractive; a realm's Event subset can enable a naval or land opportunity absent last time; a different human controls an ally; free-for-all politics can prevent a repeat of alliance lines. Positive reviewers saw high replay value in those permutations. [S09, S12–S14]

The replay ceiling is equally visible. Position, order, setup, ordinary unit statistics, map, and dungeon locations never change. Once card pools and opening attacks are known, uncertainty shifts toward which events occur rather than which strategic world exists. Richeson anticipated that the fixed board and limited tactics would be fun in smaller doses. Baker himself said Event randomness was included because fixed openings can be solved “to some extent.” [S04, S11]

Current evidence for realised afterlife is meaningful but niche:

| Form | Evidence | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contemporary recognition | Origins Best Board Game award and strongly favourable 2011 reviews | Award and launch reception do not measure repeated play. [S07–S14] |
| Current hobby record | BGG 6.9 from roughly 1,700 ratings, about 524 comments, complexity 2.64/5 on 2026-08-23 | Moderate, self-selected, mutable aggregate; no retention curve. [S17] |
| Reference and translation work | Rules aids, faction-reference redesigns, card templates, several translations, and a Polish rules upload in February 2026 | Creation/download availability is not active play. [S17] |
| Extension | Ten-dungeon campaign variant; reduced-downtime activation; Expanded TTS variant with new Guardians, Rogue, combined arms, and revised decks | Fan repair demonstrates attachment and unmet desires, not official adoption. [S17, S19] |
| Remote preservation | Tabletop Simulator objects posted in 2020 and maintained/commented on later | Pages now show removal/incompatibility warnings; small platform counts. [S19] |
| Engine reimplementation | Public 2024 TripleA work log | No finished public release demonstrated. [S20] |
| Collector persistence | Explicitly out of print by 2018 and expensive listings later | Scarcity and price are not play or demand. [S18] |

No sales total, print run, match log, completion rate, frequency-of-play survey, organised-play record, or representative abandonment sample was located. Baker's 2012 reference to thousands playing indicates reach but is informal. The evidence supports a remembered and still-tinkered-with game, not a large continuously serviced ecosystem. [S05, S17–S20]

The kinds of fan extension are themselves diagnostic. A campaign variant supplies mapped adventures and cross-box continuity that the abstract dungeons lack. A reduced-downtime variant replaces full-turn activation and allows simultaneous building. The Expanded mod adds faction and combined-arms texture and incorporates popular house rules. These projects identify what committed players wanted more of or wanted repaired; they cannot tell us how many ordinary owners shared those priorities. [S17, S19]

## 23. Solo, artificial opposition, and digital tractability

The box contains no solitaire system, neutral strategic opponent, automa deck, scripted campaign opposition, or artificial intelligence. Any solo play requires one person to control all realms with knowledge of every hidden hand. That can support rules study or exploratory tests, but it changes secrecy, alliance communication, bluffing, and adversarial inference. [S01, S16]

The public digital afterlife separates visual reproduction from rules implementation:

- Tabletop Simulator fan objects reproduce board, cards, pieces, and handling but leave players to enforce the rules.
- The TripleA attempt sought engine enforcement and immediately encountered mismatches: one global die system versus d6–d20 probabilities, First Strike, multiple attacks, Hero behaviour at sea, flying movement and landing, Castles, and other unit exceptions. [S19–S20]

This is useful evidence about the actual rules object. The game looks at first like a conventional territory-and-dice system, but its identity depends on exceptions that cross phases and environments. Heroes can fight aboard a Warship and again after landing; cargo destruction follows its ship; flying units attack and reposition; Siege Engines change dice by attacker status; Storm Elementals change dice by environment; Wizards pre-empt ordinary attacks; Dragons track a temporary hit; Castles inactivate and transfer instead of dying; Event and Treasure cards alter timing and state. [S01–S03, S20]

None of that proves that a faithful digital version or heuristic opponent would be impossible. It establishes only that a generic *Axis & Allies*-style engine did not absorb the system without custom work, and that no completed licensed computer adaptation or supported move API was located. No AI strength, search, heuristic, learning, explanation, or agent-integration evidence exists for the boxed product. [S17–S20]

## 24. The complete tension map

The most consequential virtues and frictions are paired rather than independent:

| Design move | What it buys | What it risks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed map and setup | Immediate legibility, authored fronts, fast first conflict | Solved openings and repeated scripts |
| Fixed alliances | Social permission to attack, team narrative, clearer enemies | Less diplomacy and rigid player-count structures |
| Full realm turns | Coherent offensives and simple sequence | Long waits and non-interactive first-move tempo |
| Constant 6+ threshold | Fast mixed-army resolution and tactile D&D identity | Nonlinear probabilities still need dice knowledge; exceptions accumulate |
| Shared unit roster | Low faction-learning burden | Softer faction identity and possible card dependence |
| Owner-chosen casualties | Composition, screening, preservation decisions | Cheap-unit stacks and protected high-value pieces |
| Heroes and dungeons | Adventure/war integration and alternate allocation | Abstract repetition and advantage amplification |
| Persistent Treasures | Force customisation and replay variation | Random power spikes and compounding leads |
| Action-based VP | Aggression and an end before total cleanup | Score-map decoupling and perverse liberation incentives |
| Multiple game lengths | Selectable commitment and victory meaning | Different balance regimes, not merely different durations |
| Hidden Event/Treasure hands | Surprise, faction flavour, imperfect leader knowledge | Planning volatility and card familiarity burden |
| 252-piece spectacle | Epic fantasy war made visible | Setup, clutter, cost, and handling labour |

This table is not a future-game recommendation. It describes why assessments of *Conquest of Nerath* can disagree without one side having missed the design. Many reviewers who praised speed, aggression, dice, cards, and dungeons were praising the same structures that later groups criticised for snowball, volatility, waiting, and replay limits.

## 25. Evidence limits

- Barry's reasons for treating the game as a strong influence remain intentionally unknown.
- No typical faction, alliance, game-length, or player-count win rates were found.
- The harshest repeat-play balance source mislabels the official short alliance threshold as medium.
- The opposing Good-side balance account consists of three solo multi-hand tests.
- Dragon stacking is directly confirmed as a post-release problem; other unit-efficiency claims are more contested.
- No representative duration, setup-time, downtime, mastery, abandonment, replay, or return data exist.
- Reviewers often do not state game count, chosen clock, faction, player expertise, or whether a house rule was used.
- BGG ratings, ranks, files, ownership, and comments are mutable, self-selected platform measures.
- Current asking prices establish scarcity only weakly and do not establish completed sales or active demand.
- The first-pass local map topology has not been physically validated and was not used for exact positional analysis.
- The card transcriptions were not independently compared image-by-image against a second complete public scan during this case; they are used conservatively for themes and transparent counts.
- No official errata, revised printing, expansion, sequel, reprint, licensed digital version, or completed public rules-engine port was located.
- Failure to locate one is not proof that no private implementation or obscure distribution exists.
- No AI or solo-opponent evidence exists because the shipped game has neither.

## 26. Research finding, without a future-game conclusion

*Conquest of Nerath* is not merely fantasy painted onto *Axis & Allies*. It is a deliberately compressed conquest game built around a recognisable lineage, then altered in four important ways: default narrative sides remove the social burden of choosing an arbitrary victim; enemy enclaves and action VP create immediate forward motion; polyhedral dice make power tactile while preserving one hit threshold; and Heroes convert dungeons into an alternate route whose rewards feed the war. [S01, S04]

Its simplicity is architectural. Every realm follows six steps, every ordinary attack seeks six or more, every colour shares nine piece classes, every land space produces one Gold, and every battle uses one casualty grammar. Depth accumulates through the connections among movement environments, transport, attack order, casualty protection, production geography, realm cards, persistent Treasures, dungeon allocation, alliance timing, and victory clocks. A beginner can perform a turn before knowing the full strategic consequence of those connections. [S01–S03]

The design's most successful answer is also its central unresolved tension. Scoring conquests and Treasures before total domination produces aggression, visible progress, and an end before the board must be cleaned of all resistance. But the game remembers attacks after territory changes, converts conquest into economy, preserves captured production, and lets most Treasures continue generating value. The endpoint is shortened; the snowballing engine remains. Whether that feels like welcome momentum or a foregone second half depends materially on the clock, mode, draw, and group. [S01, S03–S04, S11, S15–S16]

Its asymmetry is similarly economical rather than comprehensive. Realm decks, positions, turn slots, and compensation tell four different war stories with one unit rulebook. That lowers teaching burden and allows hidden cards to surprise. It also leaves faction personality contingent on draws and produces balance arguments that cannot be resolved without condition-specific play data. The one issue with unusually strong evidence is Dragon stacking, because both players and Baker identified it and Baker proposed a restriction. [S03–S05, S11–S16]

The afterlife shows affection without an enduring official platform. An Origins award, current ratings, translations, player aids, a campaign variant, downtime repair, virtual-tabletop work, a large fan expansion, and an attempted TripleA port demonstrate that people continued to preserve, teach, modify, and try to digitise it. Out-of-print status, no official follow-up, modest community scale, unstable fan ports, and absent retention data prevent stronger claims. [S07, S17–S20]

Those findings are retained as evidence about this case only. Barry's personal response, relationships to the other games, and any implication for the proposed browser-based fantasy wargame remain deferred until the corpus-wide fireside discussion and later comparative audit.
