# Sources: *Conquest of Nerath*

**Research date:** 2026-08-23  
**Primary scope:** Wizards of the Coast's single 2011 English-language boxed release  
**Afterlife boundary:** Unofficial aids, variants, virtual-tabletop objects, and attempted engine ports are evidence of preservation and player response, not part of the shipped game.

## Source-quality key

- **Primary:** printed rules, cards, reference material, publisher catalogue, direct designer/developer statement, or institutional award record.
- **Near-primary:** faithful transcription or player aid checked against primary material, contemporary player discussion, or current platform record.
- **Secondary:** independent contemporary review, later critical replay account, or retrospective analysis.
- **Tertiary:** database, catalogue, store, or search index used for orientation or a mutable snapshot.
- **Community claim:** self-selected player, uploader, modder, or fan statement; useful for realised practice but not representative opinion.

Barry's full rules transcription was read in full and checked against a surviving English rulebook mirror. The mirror contains fourteen PDF sheets: the game material is imposed as paired printed pages and runs through numbered page 24, followed by an added Win2PDF evaluation sheet. It is therefore safer to call the original a 24-numbered-page printed rulebook than to repeat the transcription header's “13-page rulebook” description without explanation. The compact transcription was checked against the published Universal Head v1.1 PDF.

The local primary evidence is unusually good for exact mechanics but not fully self-contained as archival provenance: its header links to processed PDF and YAML paths that were no longer present on the research date. A separately hosted rulebook scan and the public compact aid provide independent document checks. Card, setup, guardian, and map transcriptions in Barry's separate Nerath workspace are treated as companion transcriptions, not publisher-hosted originals.

## Direct design, development, publication, and award evidence

### S04 — Richard Baker and Peter Lee, “Conquest of Nerath: Design & Development” (15 June 2011)

- Type: preserved copy of an official Wizards of the Coast designer/developer article; **primary for history and design intent**.
- URL: [Google Groups preservation of the article and original Wizards link](https://groups.google.com/g/wegottapair/c/vBBgpFlrvnc).
- Used for: 2005 “Demogorgon” origin; named influence from *Risk*, *Axis & Allies*, *Empires of the Middle Ages*, *Greyhawk Wars*, *Divine Right*, and other light wargames; shelving and revival; initial setting independence and later Nentir Vale alignment; primary alliance mode and its social rationale; immediate border conflicts; desired “cast of thousands”; cut unit types; numerical development; 6+ combat and base/die memory aids; fixed-setup determinism; event-deck origin and realm differentiation; dungeon/Treasure “tech-up”; guardian redesign; anti-cleanup victory points; removed dragon bounty; and hidden Treasure scores.
- Caveat: the text is a launch article by the game's makers. It demonstrates intended problems and solutions, not that every solution succeeded in ordinary play. The original Wizards page is no longer reliably available; the preserved copy retains its link and article text.

### S05 — Richard Baker, “Conquest of Nerath, A Bad Week in Seattle” (2 June 2012)

- Type: direct designer retrospective; **primary for post-release reflection and house rule**.
- URL: [Baker's Field, June 2012 archive](https://richard-baker.blogspot.com/2012/06/).
- Used for: seven-year “labour of love”; designer satisfaction with replayability and the deterministic/random balance; approximately a month and a half of lunchtime play across positions; Peter Lee's piece/card balancing; Baker's setting regret; post-release conclusion that stacked dragons were too good; and his recommended one-friendly-dragon-per-space house rule.
- Caveat: Baker's statement that “thousands” were playing is informal reach testimony, not audited sales or active-player data. His house rule is designer advice, not printed errata.

## Later criticism and repeat-play disagreement

### S15 — Fred Manzo, The Boardgaming Way review (23 March 2014)

- Type: later repeat-play critical account; **secondary/community claim**.
- URL: [review](https://theboardgamingway.com/dungeons-and-dragons-conquest-of-nerath-a-boardgaming-way-review/).
- Used for: repeated four-player alliance experience; claimed Evil/first-turn and Nerath double-team advantage; dispersed Good positions; runaway-leader concern; dungeon farming as lead amplification; conquest-versus-liberation incentive; and proposed turn-order, scoring, and dungeon changes.
- Critical source-condition mismatch: the author says the group repeatedly used “medium” alliance conditions of 20 VP. The official rules define **20 shared VP as short** and **30 shared VP as medium**. The account remains useful evidence of their experienced pattern, but it is not a clean test of the official medium game and cannot carry a general balance verdict.

### S16 — Edward Hamilton, “Fix This Game!: Conquest of Nerath” (8 August 2014)

- Type: later solo/repeat-play analysis and house-rule essay; **secondary/community claim**.
- URL: [essay](https://wildernessoftygers.blogspot.com/2014/08/fix-this-game-conquest-of-nerath.html).
- Used for: high-quality component assessment; “two linked games” framing; three solo multi-hand tests producing Good rather than Evil wins; hypothesis that short alliance play favours Evil opening tempo while long play favours Good cards/dungeon access; medium as a possible compromise; unit-role criticism; and combined-arms, turn-order, dragon, and retreat variants.
- Quantitative correction: the essay says Fighters are statistically uniformly superior at equal cost to Siege Engines and Monsters. Official baseline arithmetic does not support the Siege claim on attack: an attacking Siege Engine rolls 2d8 for 0.75 expected hits, or 0.375 per gold, versus a Fighter's 0.50 expected hits, or 0.25 per gold. Fighter movement, Hero access, sea fighting, and Treasure synergies may still make it the more versatile purchase. The essay's conclusion is therefore treated as a play concern, not established mathematical fact.
- Caveat: three solo tests are anecdotal and remove normal team communication, negotiation, and skill variation. The opposite balance finding demonstrates uncertainty; it does not settle it.

## Evidence excluded or kept peripheral

- Wikipedia and copied retailer descriptions were used only to find stronger records.
- Search-result snippets were not used for central mechanics where the rulebook or full page was available.
- No current virtual-tabletop object was launched, and no fan-mod asset was downloaded.
- No exact sales, print-run, organised-play, match, completion, replay, or retention data were located.
- No faction win-rate claim was averaged from groups using different modes, game lengths, expertise, house rules, or multi-hand solo play.
- The local first-pass map transcription was not promoted to verified board topology.
- No VASSAL release, licensed digital adaptation, official expansion, sequel, or reprint was located; non-discovery is reported as an evidence limit, not proof of universal absence.
- Strategy advice in the separate local project was not converted into Barry's personal evaluation.

## Main uncertainties carried into the dossier

1. Typical balance by faction, alliance, game length, player count, and player experience.
2. How frequently fixed openings converge once all participants know the map and card pools.
3. Whether persistent Treasure rewards broaden force-building more often than they amplify an existing lead.
4. How much the official short, medium, and long clocks change the relative strength of first-turn tempo and delayed Good-side compensation.
5. Typical setup time, turn downtime, total duration, and tolerance across groups.
6. Typical use of the free-for-all mode relative to the primary alliance mode.
7. Commercial reach, print run, sell-through, and why no official expansion or reprint followed.
8. Representative abandonment, replay, return, and long-term ownership-to-play rates.
9. Whether any complete, legally distributable computer implementation exists outside the inspected public records.
