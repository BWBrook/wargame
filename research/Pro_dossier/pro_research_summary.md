## Fantasy wargame survey

I have assembled the full survey as an editable Markdown research dossier:
`/research/Pro_dossier/pro_research_full_dossier.md`

It contains **227 annotated entries**:

- **116 board games, campaign systems and selected mass-battle rules**, from Tony Bath’s early Hyborian campaign through *The Old King’s Crown*.
- **103 computer games**, concentrated on 1981–2006 but extending to recent grand-strategic examples.
- **8 non-fantasy comparators**, including *Diplomacy*, *Kingmaker*, *Britannia*, *History of the World* and, naturally, *The Ancient Art of War*.

Each entry gives the year, type and scale, a concise account of its most instructive features, a likely weakness or design hazard, and a research-priority rating. The dossier concludes with a 25-game first-pass shortlist, a design-problem matrix and provisional principles for a modern *War of the Lance*–like game.

## The most important historical connection

The closest antecedent to SSI’s *War of the Lance* is not merely another computer game. It is TSR’s **1986 board wargame *Dragons of Glory***, published as DL11. It presented the War of the Lance as a strategic conflict between the Dragon Highlords and Whitestone forces, including leaders, armies, neutral nations and the possibility of an alternate course to the war. SSI’s computer game was explicitly based on this design. That makes *Dragons of Glory* the logical starting point for reconstructing the design lineage: identify what SSI retained, what it automated, what it simplified and what the computer interface made more difficult than the board game. 

The commercial tabletop chronology effectively begins in the 1970s rather than the 1960s. The 1960s are better understood as its campaign prehistory, particularly Tony Bath’s persistent Hyborian campaign, which already combined rulers, characters, economics, diplomacy and tabletop battles. *Chainmail* then supplied a published fantasy mass-battle framework in 1971, followed by the much more ambitious strategic fantasy board games of the mid- and late 1970s. 

## The highest-value games for your initial dossier

| Game or series | Feature to examine closely | Principal danger to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| ***Dragons of Glory* / *War of the Lance*** | Neutral nations, Dragonlance leaders, asymmetric coalitions, seasonal campaigning and a recognisable continental war. | Coarse diplomacy and battle resolution; interface-imposed obscurity; too much dependence on scripted setting knowledge.  |
| ***Divine Right*** | Neutral kingdoms whose rulers possess individual diplomatic personalities, along with limited replacements and politically important geography. | Diplomacy can become procedural or dependent on opaque tables unless motives are clearly communicated.  |
| ***White Bear & Red Moon* / *Dragon Pass*** | Named heroes, tribal coalitions, monsters, gods and magic integrated into the strategic order of battle rather than placed in a separate adventure subsystem. | Large numbers of exceptional units and special rules can overwhelm strategic legibility.  |
| ***Demonlord*** | A comparatively compact mixture of empires, neutral forces, sorcery and summoning. It is unusually close to the likely scale of your intended game. | Random or spectacular magical effects can obscure the underlying military decisions.  |
| ***Greyhawk Wars*** | Nations and armies combined with character missions, artefacts, mercenaries and the recruitment of neutral powers. | The mission layer can become a detached bonus-generating minigame rather than an integral part of the war.  |
| ***The Lords of Midnight* / *Doomdark’s Revenge*** | Recruitable lords, personal movement through the world, independently acting characters, several factions and multiple victory routes. | Autonomous actors need intelligible motives; otherwise a living world feels arbitrary rather than organic.  |
| ***War in Middle Earth*** | Simultaneous movement of characters and armies across a recognisable narrative campaign, with events emerging from their encounters. | Real-time operation can reduce considered strategic planning and make information difficult to assimilate.  |
| ***Sword of Aragon*** | City development, taxation, recruitment, exploration, heroes and optional tactical battles in one campaign. | The tactical layer can consume playtime and expose weak AI while the economic layer turns into repetitive administration.  |
| ***Warlords* and especially *Warlords II*** | Exceptional map legibility, rapid turns, city production, stack movement, heroes, ruins, hot-seat play and readily authored scenarios. | Its economy, diplomacy and battle resolution are too thin to support a genuinely living fantasy polity without additional systems.  |
| ***Master of Magic*** | The canonical open fantasy empire game: races, cities, two planes, research, summoned creatures, heroes and tactical combat. | Scope explosion, weak strategic AI, dominant combinations and late-game micromanagement.  |
| ***Birthright: The Gorgon’s Alliance*** | Probably the fullest computer implementation of D&D realm rule: provinces, holdings, regency, diplomacy, armies, realm magic and character adventures. | It is also the clearest warning about combining too many interfaces and partially connected subsystems.  |
| ***King of Dragon Pass*** | Seasonal government, advisers with perspectives, clan relations, raids, mythic quests and events that respond to cultural and political context. | Narrative events need sufficiently transparent causality to avoid becoming disguised random punishment.  |
| ***Majesty*** | Heroes act autonomously and are influenced through rewards, buildings, markets and opportunities rather than direct commands. | Autonomous behaviour becomes frustrating when the player cannot understand why agents are refusing or choosing particular tasks.  |
| ***Kohan: Immortal Sovereigns*** | Companies rather than individual soldiers, visible supply zones and an operational scale that sharply reduces RTS micromanagement. | Excessive automation can leave the player feeling that battles are being watched rather than commanded.  |
| ***War of the Ring*, second edition** | The strongest modern example of a character quest and continental military war operating simultaneously and threatening one another. | The two victory tracks must remain mutually contestable; otherwise one side of the design becomes ornamental.  |
| ***Conquest of Nerath*** | A remarkably approachable strategic fantasy war involving infantry, monsters, heroes, siege engines, ships, dragons and dungeons across several theatres. | Its broad abstractions and considerable dice dependence limit diplomacy, logistics and operational subtlety.  |
| ***Root*** | Factions that differ not merely in modifiers but in their fundamental actions, economies, tempo and victory logic. | Every increase in systemic asymmetry multiplies teaching, balance, testing and computer-AI difficulty.  |
| ***Burning Banners*** | A current example of traditional hex-and-counter fantasy campaigning, with six asymmetric kingdoms, numerous scenarios, heroes, magic and a long campaign. | Traditional wargame procedure can generate high handling costs and make each content addition expensive to test.  |
| ***Songs of Silence*** | A recent attempt to preserve strategic army preparation while compressing battles into brief semi-automatic engagements influenced by commanders and cards. | Players who expect direct tactical control may regard the compressed battle system as insufficiently interactive.  |

## How the chronology develops

### Tabletop: experimentation in the 1970s

The first major wave was remarkably adventurous. *White Bear & Red Moon*, *Nomad Gods* and *Elric* tied warfare tightly to particular mythologies; SPI’s *War of the Ring* offered separate military, quest and combined games; *Swords & Sorcery* attempted armies, diplomacy, heroes, quests, sieges and astrologically conditioned magic in one design; and *Divine Right* made neutral kingdoms and ruler personalities central to the political map. *Beastlord* added spies, hidden movement and dummy counters, while *Wizard’s Quest* demonstrated the opposite extreme—a relatively light conquest game enlivened by orcs and a roaming dragon. 

That decade is particularly valuable because its designers had not yet settled on a standard formula. Many of the games are overcomplicated, volatile or inelegant, but they often ask more interesting questions than later designs: Can a hero change allegiance? Can an apparently neutral ruler be persuaded by personalities rather than points? Can the struggle between cosmic forces alter victory itself? Can the military game and the quest game be played separately or combined?

### Tabletop: consolidation during the 1980s and 1990s

The early 1980s produced *Titan*, *Dragon Pass*, *Barbarian Kings*, *Demonlord*, *Dragonhunt*, *Dragon Rage* and *Goblin*. These range from full strategic campaigns to focused asymmetric sieges and raids. *Warhammer Fantasy Battle* then established the dominant mass-battle miniatures lineage, while *Dragons of Glory* brought Dragonlance to the grand-strategic board. *Kings & Things* used randomly assembled, terrain-related fantasy armies, and *Mighty Empires* turned a modular map into a Warhammer campaign generator. 

The 1990s are less densely populated with strategic fantasy board games, partly because miniatures and computer games absorbed much of the genre. Nevertheless, *Greyhawk Wars*, *Hordes of the Things*, *Battle Masters*, *Man O’ War*, *Dragon Dice*, *Battlemist*, *Diskwars* and *Stratego Legends* offer useful lessons in character missions, highly abstract troop classes, introductory command systems, naval theatres, component-driven design and hidden information. 

### Tabletop: the modern revival

From about 2000 onward, fantasy strategy absorbed ideas from block wargames, Euros, card-driven games and asymmetrical area control. *Wizard Kings* used blocks and fog of war; *A Game of Thrones* made simultaneous orders and diplomacy central; *War of the Ring* perfected the dual quest-and-war structure; and *BattleLore* translated a command-card tactical framework into fantasy. 

The following wave supplied a particularly rich set of individual mechanisms: *Chaos in the Old World* for divergent faction objectives, *Small World* for decline and anti-snowball pacing, *Cyclades* for auction-controlled actions, *Runewars* for seasonal orders and hero quests, *Mage Knight* for tightly integrated hero growth and strategic conquest, and *Kemet* for purchasable asymmetrical powers and aggression-positive scoring. 

More recent designs push asymmetry and hybridisation further: *Blood Rage*, *Inis*, *Battle for Rokugan*, *Root*, *Rising Sun*, *Heroes of Land, Air & Sea*, *A War of Whispers*, *Oath*, *Bloodstones*, *The Struggle for Zorn* and *Burning Banners*. Collectively they explore productive defeat, alternative political victories, hidden orders, completely different faction economies, indirect control of empires, persistent political history, semi-co-operative existential threats and renewed interest in traditional fantasy hex wargaming. 

### Computer games: unusually fertile experimentation in the 1980s

The early computer lineage is much broader than conventional RTS history suggests. *Shattered Alliance* computerised fantasy hex warfare; *Excalibur* modelled Arthurian rule, diplomacy and military action; *Archon* linked board position to real-time duels; and *The Ancient Art of War* supplied army manoeuvre, supply, terrain, unit counters and tactical zoom. *The Lords of Midnight* and *Doomdark’s Revenge* then demonstrated that individual leaders, recruiting, personal journeys and continental warfare could coexist in one computer world. 

The decade closed with an extraordinary concentration: *War in Middle Earth*, *War of the Lance*, *Sword of Aragon*, *Warlords*, *Rings of Medusa*, *Populous* and, immediately afterward, *King’s Bounty*. Between them they established most of the principal approaches that would dominate fantasy strategy: characters and armies moving together, turn-based territorial war, state development, hero-led stacks, god-game indirection and open exploration. 

### Computer games: the 1990s golden age

The 1990s expanded each branch. The clean conquest lineage produced *Warlords II*, *Gemfire* and later *Warlords III*. The strategic-plus-tactical lineage produced *Fantasy Empires*, *Master of Magic*, *Fantasy General*, *Dragon Force*, *Lords of Magic*, *Birthright*, *Brigandine*, *Age of Wonders* and *Disciples*. The hero-army lineage moved from *King’s Bounty* to *Heroes of Might and Magic*, culminating in *Heroes III*. The real-time branch yielded *Warcraft*, *Shadow of the Horned Rat*, *Myth*, *Dark Omen*, *Populous: The Beginning* and *Total Annihilation: Kingdoms*. 

Two less conventional games may be especially valuable. *King of Dragon Pass* treats culture, advisers, mythology, diplomacy and seasonal decisions as the heart of strategy, while *Seven Kingdoms II* combines economy, diplomacy, espionage, loyalty and war with non-human kingdoms. Both demonstrate that strategic fantasy can be about maintaining a society rather than simply optimising armies. 

### Computer games: 2000–2006 and beyond

The early 2000s split between increasingly hero-heavy RTS games and more interesting experiments in command abstraction. *Majesty* allowed autonomous heroes to respond to incentives; *Kohan* replaced individual-unit micro with companies and supply zones; *Dominions* embraced simultaneous grand strategy, immense faction variety and pre-scripted battles; and *Battle for Middle-earth II* placed an RTS battle engine beneath a turn-based War of the Ring campaign map. 

For later grand strategy, the dossier follows *Eador*, *King Arthur: The Role-playing Wargame*, *Elemental* and *Fallen Enchantress*, *Age of Wonders III and 4*, *Endless Legend*, *Thea*, the *Total War: Warhammer* trilogy, later *Dominions*, the modern *Master of Magic*, *SpellForce: Conquest of Eo*, *Songs of Conquest* and *Songs of Silence*. These are included selectively because they continue to address genuinely strategic questions rather than merely offering tactical combat or RPG progression. 

## Design principles emerging from the survey

1. **Keep the strategic map primary.** A detailed battle engine can easily consume most of the playtime, slow the campaign and expose tactical AI limitations. Detailed battles should probably be optional or reserved for pivotal encounters.

2. **Use heroes as strategic agents rather than miniature CRPG protagonists.** Their most useful functions are command, diplomacy, recruitment, scouting, sabotage, morale, political legitimacy and quest resolution. Incremental equipment inventories are unlikely to justify their development and interface costs.

3. **Make neutral nations actors rather than prizes.** Their decisions should depend on geography, threats, leaders, rivalries, previous treatment and the apparent course of the war. A visible but uncertain diplomatic model would improve considerably on a simple accumulation of influence points.

4. **Allow military and narrative goals to change one another.** A quest should draw away leaders or troops, open alliances, weaken an enemy, alter political legitimacy or create a temporary military opportunity—not merely award an isolated bonus.

5. **Ground economics in the setting.** Settlements, roads, ports, seasons, population, loyalty, supply and replacement capacity are more evocative than generic harvesting of wood, gold and ore.

6. **Treat magic as scarce rule-breaking.** A few strategically momentous and counterable spells will probably produce more drama than hundreds of small numerical modifiers.

7. **Build faction identity from different decisions.** One faction might depend on alliances, another on terror and rapid conquest, another on defensive mobilisation, another on mobile nomads, and another on controlling magical sites. Simple percentage bonuses do not provide the same identity.

8. **Address snowballing explicitly.** Decline, political exhaustion, threatened coalitions, finite campaign seasons, threshold victories, productive losses and escalating common threats are all preferable to a long, foregone mopping-up phase.

9. **Design for Codex-assisted iteration from the outset.** Factions, units, events and scenarios should be data-driven; randomness should be seedable; turns should be replayable from logs; and the strategic engine should support headless simulations. Those choices will matter as much as any individual combat mechanic.

The most productive next stage is a scored comparison matrix for the 25-game shortlist, with columns for map structure, army scale, hero functions, diplomacy, neutral powers, economy, supply, magic, battle resolution, victory conditions, AI feasibility and implementation risk.
