## 2026-08-23 — Case 002 completed: *Victory in the Pacific*

- User identification: Avalon Hill's 1977 *Victory in the Pacific*.
- Identity resolved: a Richard Hamblen theatre-level naval design, developed from the area-control foundation of *War at Sea* but expanded into a separate eight-turn, 13-area Pacific campaign.
- Scope selected: the 1977 first edition is primary. The clearer 1981 second-edition rules are used only where they express the common system; their revisions are identified rather than silently attributed backwards.
- Independent research completed using the original and revised rules, Hamblen's contemporary design account and later interview, Avalon Hill development history, official clarification and award records, contemporary reception references, retrospective reviews, competing strategy articles, probability analyses, variants, replays, and organised-play records through 2025.

- Praise documented: radical whole-theatre compression; high interaction; meaningful commitment timing; strategic geography; distinct Japanese and Allied arcs; memorable named-ship losses; easy resolution with an optional deep analytical layer; extensive replay and counter-strategy.
- Friction documented: layered dice variance; repetitive large-battle procedure; reduced Allied agency in the historical opening; dense and ambiguous short rules; dated physical presentation; severe historical abstraction; perfect reinforcement knowledge; a substantial novice/expert gap; and longer actual sessions than the “introductory” label may imply.

- Longevity evidence is unusually strong but niche-specific: the Charles S. Roberts Award for Best Strategic Game of 1977, extensive *The General* literature, dedicated probability/variant archives, annual organised-play lineage from 1991 through 2025, and repeated play-by-email competition.

## 2026-08-23 — Case 003 completed: *Afrika Korps*

- User identification: Avalon Hill's 1964 *Afrika Korps* (published spelling confirmed).
- Scope selected: the 1964 first edition is primary, but it is separated carefully from the 1964–65 public repair process and the mature 1980 rules tradition. Later rules are used to reconstruct common mechanisms only when identified as later evidence.
- Central edition finding: mobile, capturable, expendable supply was part of the original design, but the familiar random Axis supply-arrival table was introduced as a July 1964 suggestion and incorporated into the 1965 revision. Contemporary sources conflict over first-turn Axis supply, so no single later convention was projected back onto every early game.
- Independent research completed using first-year *General* launch issues and rulings, Avalon Hill's institutional history, the mature rules reconstruction and rewrite account, contemporary reception, competing expert strategy and variant essays, a modern tournament primer, event records through 2025, and current digital maintenance evidence.
- Central mechanisms documented: the long coastal corridor and road; physical supply as action budget, position, target, and capturable resource; mandatory ZOC combat and soak-offs; Automatic Victory during movement; Rommel's mobility bonus; isolation; Tobruk as fortress, port, and replacement hinge; the Axis deadline; and asymmetric campaign arcs.
- Praise documented: immediate board-state legibility; unusually economical logistics; manoeuvre and indirect threat before battle; meaningful scarcity; strong side identity through shared rules; easy procedure with a deep positional ceiling; coherent campaign stories; and a long culture of counter-analysis.
- Friction documented: an underdeveloped first release; high-leverage supply and combat dice; potentially campaign-deciding Tobruk rolls; sacrificial soak-offs; ambiguous rules and tribal knowledge; scripted opening theory; severe historical abstraction; perfect information; dated physical and representational framing; and a possible six-to-seven-hour expert campaign despite a shorter advertised duration.

The original release drew immediate and contradictory repairs; the settled classic is often judged close to even by experts but remains knowledge-sensitive, format-sensitive, and exposed to high-variance individual results. Tournament handicaps use Axis supply as the principal balance lever.
- Longevity evidence is strong but niche-specific: near-immediate magazine debate, extensive strategy literature, a publisher 100,000-plus sales claim, annual organised play from 1991 through 2025 apart from the 2020–21 interruption, repeated email tournaments, current teaching material, and maintained digital modules.

- Praise documented: an unusually broad whole-continent fantasy command role; strong integration of setting with strategic capabilities; a recognisable Highlord-expansion/Whitestone-coalition-and-reversal arc; consequential diplomacy; interdependence among field armies, politics, quests, magic, and subversion; configurable balance; hot-seat play; and a computer opponent contemporary reviewers found challenging.

- Reception documented from C64-specific *Zzap!64*, *The Games Machine*, *Dragon*, and *Compute!* coverage, plus contemporary *QuestBusters*, later strategy and adverse-player accounts, genre histories, sales research, preservation records, and current distribution. ## 2026-08-23 — Case 006 completed: *Warlords* and *Warlords II*

- User identification: SSG's *Warlords* (1990) and closely allied successor *Warlords II* (1993), to be treated as one coherent evolutionary package. The later branch was explicitly excluded.
- Scope selected: the common-rule 1990 original and 1993 IBM/DOS sequel are primary. The 1994 Macintosh port, separate 1994 Scenario Builder, and 1995 *Warlords II Deluxe* are retained only as afterlife evidence. This prevents the frequently repeated but incorrect attribution of a scenario editor, PBEM, modem, or network play to the base 1993 release.
- Platform status: Barry has not yet identified which platform he remembers. The dossier therefore separates common mechanics from Amiga, DOS, and Macintosh presentation and performance rather than inventing a primary remembered object.
- Independent research completed using both original manuals, rendered page inspection, SSG developer notes and launch reports, direct Steve Fawkner recollection, Australian museum objects, contemporary *Computer Gaming World*, *Compute!*, Amiga, and Usenet reception, later complete playthroughs, fan FAQs and scenario records, an international tournament archive, and current commercial catalogues.
- Origin and authorship documented: Steve Fawkner's concept and Amiga programming; Roger Keating's AI design and programming; Stephen Hart's IBM development; Gregor Whiley's production and additional development; and the sequel's joint design credit to Fawkner, Keating, Whiley, and Ian Trout. Fawkner's stated reference points were *Dragons of Glory*, *Empire*, and *The Lords of Midnight*.
- Original-game mechanisms documented: one fixed eighty-city Illuria map; eight positionally asymmetric empires; one-to-eight human/computer mixing; a real-game Elvallie tutorial; the city-capture income/production loop; production upkeep and treasury loss; local army lists and vectoring; towers, defence, and razing; terrain movement and navies; eight-unit stacks; sequential decisive combat against as many as thirty-two city defenders; heroes, ruins, fourteen artefacts, allies, and blessings; four fair-rule AI levels; and one-way AI attitudes.
- Victory correction: the objective is all eighty cities, not an automatic win at forty-one. In a one-human-versus-seven-computers game, more than forty cities can trigger a conditional surrender only when the strongest computer is also at least fifteen cities behind. Acceptance is optional; refusal unites the survivors.
- Sequel evolution documented: clean-slate code rewrite; higher-resolution and lower-dialog interface; direct stack palette, contextual cursors, shortcuts, and help; persistent pathfinding and automatic sea crossings; five normal scenarios across twenty, forty, and eighty cities plus a six-city tutorial; saveable random maps; layered information, neutral, combat, quest, and diplomatic options; twenty-eight army types; production investment, plantable vector standards, and occupy/pillage/sack/raze choices; visible and simplified bonuses; veteran medals; hero experience and quests; explicit diplomacy and credibility; named AI personalities; and open Enhanced Production handicaps.
- AI evidence was bounded carefully. Both generations document same-rule heuristic opponents, and the sequel links improved decisions to a larger state database and reliable pathfinding. Contemporary independent reviewers found the opposition unusually strong and capable of winning. No code, benchmarks, search-depth account, adaptive learning, or controlled win-rate evidence was found.
- Praise documented: a quickly grasped territorial/economic loop; eight-way strategic geometry; geography and local production that reward repeated positional learning; meaningful fantasy units and hero expeditions; credible solo play; hot-seat stories; sequel options that allow detail and uncertainty to be added selectively; improved movement and interface; random-map replay; and within-game attachment to heroes and veteran troops.
- Friction documented: fixed-map exhaustion; long sequential turns and AI waits; late-empire city, vector, and stack administration; automatic no-retreat combat with memorable but sometimes arbitrary-looking upsets; limited original diplomacy and hero progression; sequel setup burden; only five supplied normal sequel scenarios; no base editor or remote play; random-order double moves; diplomatic reputation spirals; snowball and cleanup; no cross-scenario continuity; and a notably slight victory presentation after a long campaign.
- Longevity evidence is strongest for a later extended ecosystem rather than measurable typical retention. Contemporary awards and “addictive” accounts support immediate attraction; the 1994 Scenario Builder, 1995 *Deluxe*, large fan archive, scenario and probability tools, PBEM, and a tournament with more than 250 entrants from more than twenty-five countries demonstrate realised long-term use of the *Warlords II* framework. Those facilities are not back-projected into 1993.
- Current preservation documented: ACMI and NFSA hold original Australian objects; Museums Victoria holds a 1994 Macintosh sequel package; GOG and Steam currently sell *Warlords* paired with *Warlords II Deluxe*, not the original 1993 sequel.

## 2026-08-23 — Case 007 completed: *Panzer General*, *Fantasy General*, and *Panzer Corps*

- User identification: SSI's *Panzer General* (1994), SSI's *Fantasy General* (1996), and the original *Panzer Corps* (2011), to be treated as one interrelated bundle.
- Scope selected: the DOS originals of *Panzer General* and *Fantasy General* and the Windows base release *Panzer Corps: Wehrmacht* are primary. Original ports, the intermediate Five-Star games, later SSI sequels, *Fantasy General II*, and *Panzer Corps 2* are outside the mechanical core. *Panzer Corps* DLC, Gold, editor culture, community scenarios, and organised play are retained only as labelled afterlife evidence.
- Lineage resolved: *Panzer General* was an unusually collective SSI Special Projects Group project inspired in part by the Japanese Mega Drive/Genesis *Daisenryaku*. *Fantasy General* directly shares the group credit, programmer Paul Murray, artist Dave Jensen, and several production/data staff. *Panzer Corps* is a spiritual successor rather than an SSI sequel; Alex Shargin's free *Panzer General Forever* reconstruction was its documented foundation.
- Authorship was kept distributed rather than assigned to one famous name. Joel Billings designed the original campaign and Chuck Kroegel the scenarios; Chris Carr and Chris Lanka designed the fantasy campaign; Shargin receives main design for *Panzer Corps*, with Kresimir Cuturic and Frank Leone on scenario design and research.
- Independent research completed using all three manuals, rendered page inspection, *Computer Gaming World*'s 1994 *Panzer General* preview and 1996 *Fantasy General* review, developer interviews and forum statements, later archival history, sharply conflicting *Fantasy General* reviews, three independent launch reviews of *Panzer Corps*, detailed AI and replacement-rule launch discussions, a later puzzle/snowball retrospective, official current product records, commercial preservation logs, and fan-project evidence.
- Shared grammar documented: turn-limited hex scenarios; one ground/naval and one air layer; move-and-attack activation in chosen order; class relationships and combined arms; objectives and fog; local fuel/ammunition; combat forecasts; core versus auxiliary forces; experience; veteran preservation; one campaign currency; equipment or roster development; and performance-conditioned campaign progression.
- *Panzer General* mechanisms documented: a guided real Poland scenario; five campaign entry points; an Axis-only branching campaign; nameable persistent core; prestige; ordinary versus elite replacements; the printed automatic ordinary between-battle replacement rule and conflicting program reports; experience stars and overstrength; initiative, suppression, entrenchment, rugged defence, support fire, weather, spotting, zones of control, local supply, and aircraft fuel loss; hot-seat and final-release PBM file exchange.
- Replacement-rule discrepancy: the 1994 manual says understrength core units receive ordinary zero-experience replacements between campaign scenarios. Players in both 1997 and the 2011 *Panzer Corps* launch discussion report that at least some copies instead preserved experience. This unresolved manual/program or version conflict is retained. *Panzer Corps* unambiguously removes automatic replacement: green deployment replacements are free but dilute experience, while paid elite replacements preserve it.
- *Fantasy General* transformation documented: four materially asymmetric commanders; ten classes and four races; percentage-based research and unit grades; physical, magical, and mechanical attacks; melee, skirmish, missile, and siege ranges; wounds versus kills; Rest versus Recruit; morale, retreat, surrender, encirclement, and flanking; magic, items, heroes, and Mech repair; continent-route choice; campaign-ending battle loss; and a substantial configurable Arena with random maps, saved/imported armies, custom conditions, and PBM.
- AI terminology was constrained. *Fantasy General* Campaign difficulty adjusts enemy gold and unit experience; Arena “AI Level” explicitly means purchased-unit experience. No evidence supports several smarter reasoning systems. The 1996 reviewer nevertheless observed effective target selection and rear attacks.
- *Panzer Corps* reconstruction documented: a separate six-scenario tutorial plus contextual help; five campaign starts and twenty-six-scenario tree; reserve/deployment roster; manual replacement choice; medals, heroes, and bonus SS units; mass attack, close defence, switchable roles, and richer prediction; conditional undo; integrated PBEM++; shipping developer-grade editor; and exposed scenario, equipment, campaign, rule, and UI data.
- *Panzer Corps* AI evidence remains mixed and granular. Launch reviewers praised tactical target selection and punishment of exposed units; players documented weak operational concentration, suppression order, finishing, and river crossings; Shargin stated that AI work was unfinished. No source code, learning system, search-depth account, or controlled performance data was found.
- Praise documented: unusually low action-level friction with optional statistical depth; visible combined-arms logic; persistent personalised units; prestige/gold choices that link battles; short scenarios within larger careers; strong tutorial and interface work; commander/research variety in the fantasy adaptation; and progressively lower-friction human play and authorship from manual PBM through Arena to PBEM++ and an editor.
- Friction documented: elastic historical scale and simplified supply; turn limits that can turn scenarios into route puzzles; advance-knowledge and reload incentives; compounding prestige/experience snowball; painful veteran loss; limited or misleading difficulty controls; fixed heuristic opponents; underdeveloped fantasy magic and generic story; delayed punishment from research allocation; PBM file labour; dated and repetitive presentation; and German/Axis career framing that abstracts ideology and atrocity.
- Reception was not collapsed to a verdict. *Fantasy General* is the sharpest disagreement: *Computer Gaming World* awarded five stars and called it the true successor, while GameSpot saw a shallow reskin and *Arcane* supplied a specific adverse “one more go” judgement. *Panzer Corps* received strong launch scores but also price, AI, presentation, and scenario-puzzle criticism.
- Longevity evidence was separated by product. *Panzer General* had exceptional commercial and critical impact but no current original commercial re-release was located. *Fantasy General* is actively preserved by GOG/SNEG and has a small, strongly favourable modern review audience. *Panzer Corps* has the largest realised ecosystem, but current Gold reviews, hundreds of scenarios, mods, editor use, PBEM++, and eighteen bundled items must not be projected back into the 2011 base box.

## 2026-08-23 — Case 008 completed: *V for Victory: Gold-Juno-Sword*

- Scope selected: the 1993 DOS and Macintosh release is primary. - Independent research completed using the original Operations Manual, the 1995 consolidated Reference Manual, rendered manual pages, DOS screenshots, Eric Young and Keith Zabalaoui's pre-release design account, Keith Zabalaoui's later Atomic interview, contemporary *Computer Gaming World*, *Electronic Entertainment*, *High Score Express*, Game Bytes, *Compute!*, *PC Format*, and PC Gamer records, period Usenet discussion, later player criticism, databases, and current preservation records.

The landings are deliberately omitted because divisional and corps commanders had little real-time agency during the scripted assault. That defensible simulation boundary became the central contemporary dramaturgical criticism.
- Scale and command object documented: one kilometre per hex, four hours per turn, predominantly battalion/company ground units, functional headquarters, three Allied corps, off-map mission assignment for air and naval power, and a bounded operational contest over Caen and the inland road network rather than production or strategic research.

- Ground systems documented: tactical and strategic movement, passenger and automated movement, stacks and divisional orders, bridges and engineers, zones of control, attachments, regimental integrity, stacking, optional one-division attacks, probe through all-out-assault postures, retreat/defend/hold-at-all-costs defence, digging in, and fortification.

- Supply documented as a daily operational network: five 0600 headquarters priorities, formation-dependent tonnage, beaches and stockpiles, trace length, roads, terrain, zones of control, interdiction, weather, attachments, progressive out-of-supply states, abundant Commonwealth capacity, and heavier British artillery consumption. Contemporary players praised the depth but wanted proactive exception warnings rather than having to find supply failures through overlays and inspection.
- Information and condition systems documented: Limited Intelligence for the enemy, unusually including optional incomplete reports about one's own formations through Fog of War; close and zoomed views; overlays; weather; morale; fatigue; disruption; replacements; fortifications; and unit/HQ status displays.
- Victory and variant systems documented: objectives scored through time as well as at scenario end; casualties; ten-point Allied penalties for British replacements; date- and ship-dependent naval-use penalties; six result bands; the historical campaign classified as a Marginal German Victory through Allied delay; plausible changes to experience, German reinforcement timing and position, air balance, weather, realism, hidden side variants, and handicap.
- Scenario-count discrepancy retained: the Operations Manual alternates among seven scenarios, six basic scenarios, and six listed scenarios; Game Bytes reports seven. No false clean count or speculative complete scenario list was created. *The Orne Bridges*, *The Campaign Game*, and a reference to *Charge of the Bull* are the only names used.
- Staff assistance documented separately from opposing AI. The player can delegate daily supply, fire planning, unassigned ground movement, all operations, and night handling, often inspect an immediate assistant plan, revise parts, or replace it. Automatic movement and divisional control are additional layers. This is evidence of selectable administrative involvement, not evidence that every delegated plan is competent.
- Artificial-opponent evidence remains explicitly mixed. Atomic's preview claimed a more decisive 4.0 opponent with better headquarters, artillery, and supply handling. *Computer Gaming World* reported serious failures of coordination, exploitation, regrouping, and replacements; Game Bytes found generally acceptable but sometimes foolish play; Usenet reports range from fixes over earlier releases to severe criticism. No source code, search-depth account, learning mechanism, planning trace, or controlled win-rate evidence was found.
- Interface and teaching documented: mouse/menu operation, military or pictorial counters, contextual panels, operational overlays, strategic locator, victory and weather displays, substantial Operations/Reference documentation, and a guided first two turns of *The Orne Bridges*. Positive reviewers found board-wargame complexity unexpectedly manageable; adverse evidence includes small text, scrolling, manual dependence, lack of alerts and multi-selection, batch-order burden, and editorial/version residue.
- Human-play boundary documented: computer opposition and local hot-seat play with separate private planning and common execution information. No modem, network, PBEM, or base editor shipped, despite a long campaign for which contemporary reviewers wanted remote play.
- Save limitation documented: saves retain static state, destinations, and targets rather than every exact route, report, and transient order. Resumption reconstructs paths and may alter them; autosave overwrites the preceding autosave.
- Reception was not averaged into a verdict. *Electronic Entertainment* awarded 8/10, *High Score Express* 4/5, and Game Bytes strongly recommended the game to serious wargamers. *Computer Gaming World* praised technology, manuals, aids, and interface but issued a severe review over the missing landings, lopsided campaign, AI, duration, and absent modem play; the same magazine later named the game a Wargame of the Year finalist for its artillery, supply, and interface refinements.

Engine quality, campaign attraction, staff-assistant usefulness, and enemy-AI competence are treated as separate questions.
- Longevity evidence is modest and selected: a roughly sixty-hour campaign estimate, variants, four-game engine compatibility, a 1995 collection, a later Hall of Belated Fame listing, individual intensive-play recollections, preservation activity, and a small GOG wishlist. No completion telemetry, representative retention sample, major editor culture, or current official GOG/Steam release was located.

- Turn-scale uncertainty preserved: the 1914 game was reported as 118 turns and a detailed iPad review says two-week turns; a later HistoryNet review says weekly; the original manual is silent. Approximately fortnightly is treated as reconstruction, not exact primary fact.
- Player role documented: whole-coalition command over land, air, and naval forces plus production, finite manpower, ammunition, rail and sea capacity, research, commanders, diplomacy, national morale, and surrender.
- Core IGOUGO action grammar documented: move then attack, all remaining movement consumed by attack, independent advance after clearing a hex, adjacent swaps, limited rail redeployment, and ranged units choosing between move and attack.
- Entrenchment documented as an automatic **hex state**, not a unit possession. Idle land units build it to the national technology cap; terrain multiplies its value; swaps inherit it; vacant works decay one level per turn; capture reduces rather than erases high fortification.
- Launch/mature entrenchment difference documented: in version 1.1.2 being attacked interrupted entrenchment; later patches allowed it to continue unless retreat occurred.
- Defensive activity documented: place-based works, reserve swaps, supply, efficiency recovery, and quiet-sector rest make a stable line a maintained asset rather than inert counters awaiting another turn.
- Efficiency documented as the unifying readiness state for fatigue, morale, cohesion, and combat capability. Movement, attack, hostile territory, winter, and adverse combat reduce it; full/half supply restores five/three; quality caps it; low efficiency creates vulnerability before nominal strength is gone.
- Serial combined-arms grammar documented: ranged Shock and Bombard reduce efficiency, strength, terrain, or entrenchment; infantry attacks afterward; cavalry exploits retreat; developed armour and fast units enter or widen the breach.
- Ammunition documented as a separate national resource. Artillery is powerful, non-retaliatory, immobile when firing, and expensive in shells; ammunition-factory investment and a developed stockpile cap make an offensive a staged national project.
- Research documented across ground, artillery, naval, air, and vehicle categories. Upkeep-bearing laboratories accelerate a category; one technology can be focused at the expense of the rest; focus points constrain switching; completed research still requires paid unit upgrades.
- Trench-warfare finding: engagement lies primarily in selecting, funding, preparing, sequencing, and exploiting a rare offensive, while other fronts and the national economy remain active—not in making repetitive unsupported infantry attacks individually entertaining.
- Mobility arc documented: unentrenched 1914 fronts and more open eastern terrain permit movement; mid-war technology and fortification favour attrition; improved infantry Assault, artillery Bombard/Shock, developed armour, reserves, and advance moves create later breach windows.
- Barry's stormtrooper recollection bounded: the 1918 start is *Kaiserschlacht*, but the launch manual exposes no dedicated stormtrooper unit or infiltration phase. Late infiltration/combined arms are abstracted through setup, Assault improvements, bombardment, shock, armour, and serial attack order.
- Bounded breakthrough documented: clearing a defender enables one advance independent of movement already spent, while armoured cars scout/cut supply, cavalry punishes retreat, and late armour breaks lines; there is no unrestricted multi-combat exploitation cascade.
- Production/manpower feedback documented: manpower pays for units, falling reserves reduce PP income, and threshold declines reduce unit quality. Repairing casualties therefore weakens national capacity rather than drawing from an infinite replacement pool.
- Upkeep and national collapse documented: forces and laboratories consume PP, deficit lowers national morale, city/capital-ship losses and bombardment matter politically, and zero morale produces a surrender offer.
- Supply documented: controlled land connections to capitals, cities, qualifying fortresses, or adjacent surface fleets create full, half, or no-supply states. Half supply cuts movement and recovery; no supply limits movement to one, prevents repair/recovery, and removes one strength per turn.
- Convoy linkage documented: maritime arrivals carry production and sometimes manpower; submarine interdiction weakens the Entente but shifts US alignment. Naval decisions therefore affect continental force sustainability and diplomacy.
- Unit roles documented across garrisons, infantry, cavalry, armoured cars, armour, artillery, railroad guns, fighters, bombers, airships, surface fleets, submarines, and convoys. Exact advertised roster count varies between launch and later product copy and was not forced into a timeless number.
- Air mechanism documented from the original rules: early fighters deliberately reduce ground efficiency before land attacks; bombers and airships deliberately conduct long-range strategic bombardment; strategic attack damages city production; air units remain in full supply.
- Developed air tactic documented: an officially pinned guide teaches fighter-first attacks even without strength damage and recommends a serial order beginning with fighters and airships before artillery, bombers, and ground formations.
- Barry's air-power concern independently corroborated: players describe grossly overpowered zeppelins and extreme bomber/air effects, although these reports are self-selected and span versions.
- Official acknowledgement documented through patches: bomber/airship attacks received higher ammunition costs; fighters were made able to intercept attacks on empty cities; cities recovered faster; bombers were weakened; early fighters were reframed as reconnaissance; and v1.6.2 explicitly toned down all air units because this was World War I.
- Air failure classified as relational: historically labelled reconnaissance and strategic bombing became dominant through their interaction with efficiency, full-supply status, range, attack order, interception, ammunition economics, and city recovery—not simply through an incorrect unit name.
- Alliance viability documented without claiming statistical parity: distinct star conditions and temporal asymmetry give Central Powers early concentration/knockout opportunities and the Entente coalition, convoy, naval, entrant, and long-war advantages.
- Community balance disagreement retained: early reports found campaigns poised between gain and collapse; one multiplayer account saw a sharp shift toward Entente advantage by late 1915; another thought the orthodox continental war was broadly balanced. No win-rate telemetry was located.
- Diplomacy documented as alignment drift, events, declarations, and action-driven consequences rather than rich negotiation. More than forty historical/what-if events were advertised, but reviewers found political agency and historical granularity shallower than competing grand-strategy games.
- Visual/interface evidence documented: illustrated sprites or counters, visible trench layers, terrain-production inspection, minimap, tooltips, combat forecasts for strength and efficiency, repair mode, event summaries, and high-resolution/widescreen support attracted repeated praise.
- Teaching friction retained: simple immediate verbs coexist with a seventy-four-page manual, a later official FAQ for underquantified mechanics, and mobile criticism of a weak tutorial and absent in-app rules.
- Global workload documented: whole-alliance control across multiple fronts and management panels can make turns long; one iPad reviewer reported twenty-plus minutes for the player and about five for the AI.
- AI authorship and boundary documented: David Forster is credited; a development-period statement describes a new opponent intended to react to player action rather than preset global conditions; no internal algorithm or search architecture was found.
- AI difficulty documented as resources only: Balanced gives no advantage, Handicapped fewer AI resources, Privileged more. The easiest launch setting initially contained a reversed bonus bug.
- AI strengths retained: exploiting gaps, pressing several fronts, executing recognisable openings, concentrating attacks, and using fighter-first sequences. AI weaknesses retained: learnable openings, theatre redistribution, Italy/Russia/naval handling, support exposure, build choices, long loops, and crashes.
- Patch-level AI changes documented: improved Austro-Hungarian and German openings, Gallipoli attempt, submarine construction, last-survivor defence, naval behaviour, performance, and repeated crash/loop fixes. AI quality was not treated as a static single score.
- Sustained-engagement evidence bounded: launch “one more turn” session reports, play into early morning, RPS praise halfway through 118 turns, a seven-hour iPad session reaching turn fifteen, GrogHeads' 2012 reader award, detailed guides, PBEM, long-hour Steam reviews, and continued mod labour.
- Potzblitz afterlife documented separately: thousands of forum replies and active 2025–26 fixes show enduring attachment and a productive modding substrate, but the modified object cannot validate vanilla balance, AI, or retention.
- Additional adverse findings documented: dominant build mixes, shallow diplomacy, generic formations, weaker naval play, disband/refund exploits, accidental moves, forecast/save-reload temptation, repetitive global administration, fixed-map campaign similarity, thin endgame feedback, and ended official support.
- Current preservation status documented: the Steam listing remains purchasable and mostly positive but explicitly warns that official developer support ended and the last patch was in 2015. Current reviews mix versions and are not launch evidence.
- Central research finding: *Commander: The Great War* makes stalemate playable by converting it into an active resource-tempo system—fortification, readiness, reserve rotation, ammunition, manpower, supply, research, and national morale—punctuated by expensive but real breakthrough windows.

## 2026-08-24 — Case 013 completed: *Dragons of Glory*

- User identification: TSR’s DL11 *Dragons of Glory*, a 1986 continent-scale Dragonlance board wargame featuring the Dragon Highlords, Whitestone, neutral nations, leaders, and an alternate-history strategic campaign; proposed as the direct tabletop precursor to SSI’s *War of the Lance*.
- Barry’s statement was quarantined in barry-context.md. The title established scope, while the lineage and mechanical importance of the named features were tested independently.
- Product boundary resolved: the primary object is the eight-page standard game plus sixteen-page campaign/source booklet, maps, counters, cover tables, and strategic events sold in DL11. The separately published March 1986 *Dragon* 107 advanced system is analysed as a related alternate mode, not silently merged into the box.
- Release chronology resolved conservatively: the printed work is copyright 1986 and *Dragon* 105 listed it among January 1986 shipments; several catalogues and a museum record say 1985, probably reflecting production or solicitation rather than a securely demonstrated retail year.
- Original credits documented: Douglas Niles and Tracy Raye Hickman; Mike Breault editing; Jeff Easley cover; Jeff Butler and Diana Magnuson interiors; David Sutherland III, Steven D. Sullivan, and Picard Didier Inc. cartography; Linda Bakk and Carolyn Vanderbilt typography; Colleen O’Malley keylining.
- Physical object documented: two-part full-colour Ansalon hex map, about 400 counters, cover charts, eight-page rules, sixteen-page scenario/history/order-of-battle booklet, and a small counter bag.
- Product-origin evidence documented: DL13’s questionnaire says respondents to the DL5 survey requested a Dragonlance board game and TSR designed DL11 in response. No results survive, so novice-audience success was not inferred.
- Three-scale identity documented: AD&D characters at quest scale, BATTLESYSTEM at medium battle scale, and DL11 at continent/army scale. DL11 is self-contained; the bridges among scales are optional and loosely procedural.
- Grand-strategic boundary documented: political, geographic, military, and narrative development without taxation, resource production, construction queues, city building, or a technology tree.
- Two campaigns documented: a thirty-turn Full Campaign beginning in March 348 AC before Highlord conquest or Whitestone mobilisation, and a shorter but equally complex Historical Campaign beginning on Turn 20 from a saga-adjacent political and military state.
- Time scale documented: two months per ordinary standard turn and four months per winter turn, with the printed track ending in winter 353 AC. Designers estimate six to eight hours for experienced full-campaign play.
- Four base scenarios documented: *The Fall of Silvanesti*, *The Solamnic Plain*, *The Kender Strike*, and *The Siege of Kalaman*.
- Tutorial intent documented: the booklet orders scenarios by fictional chronology but recommends learning in the order scenario 3, scenario 1, scenario 2, then scenario 4 for a compact all-rules contest.
- Alternate-history support documented as structurally real: activation, invasion response, initiative, event order, battle, and conquest can rewrite the political and military timeline from 348 AC; the Historical Campaign supports later divergence.
- Standard turn documented: replacements, strategic event, activation, initiative, first side move/combat, second side move/combat, and time advancement.
- Initiative documented as order control: opposing d4 rolls, ties retaining the current holder, and the winner choosing which coalition acts first; move-second/move-first sequences can create consecutive operations across turns.
- Neutral activation documented as the force-generation system: one side-specific d10 attempt per coalition per turn; activation adds a complete national force, opens territory, creates objectives, and changes routes.
- Invasion politics documented: violating neutral territory, including flyover, forces alternating activation rolls and advantages the non-invader, making aggression capable of delivering the target nation to the enemy.
- Threat-driven Knight mobilisation documented: their Whitestone activation chances improve with Highlord conquest/alliance, and first Knight activation deploys the High Clerist Tower armies.
- Movement model documented: national/racial terrain differences, tight two-ground-counter stacking, three in fortified cities, kender non-stacking, contact stopping, cavalry/leader exemptions, flying forces, naval transport and bridges, wizard relocation, and flying-citadel lift.
- Maelstrom documented as a compact push-your-luck strategic feature: sinking, delay, opponent-selected exit, or owner-selected exit.
- Combat documented: adjacent single-target multi-hex attacks, odds CRT on a d10, leader/dragon/wizard/mobile/terrain/fortification modifiers, two-step armies, retreat, elimination, advance, and extra mobile exploitation.
- Dragon leverage documented: dragon strength changes both odds and die result; dragonlances double a carrier army and suppress the dragon modifier, creating the central early-dominance/later-reversal arc.
- Strategic-event system documented: alternating draws, E1/E10/E20 staging, activation bonuses, wizards, dragonlances, orbs, Golden General, good dragons, gnomish technology, flying citadels, and Lord Soth.
- Standard leader boundary documented: leaders are principally tactical modifiers and item users; the base rules omitted a general fate rule when their whole stack was destroyed.
- Naval system documented: simultaneous d10 checks against ratings, damaged and sunk sides, repeated rounds, carried-army and leader fate, and permanent loss of ships.
- Attrition documented: two eliminated national counters return one full army and permanently remove the other; two depleted stacked armies consolidate similarly; special air and naval units are irreplaceable.
- Draconian production documented: the Highlord creates one army per replacement step at the Dark Temple up to the counter limit; capturing the temple ends production/replacement there.
- Conquest cascade documented: capture of every fortified site removes the country’s land/air armies and leaders permanently, while ships remain; Knight realms use a federated collapse condition.
- Rear-area significance documented: ungarrisoned fortified sites plus fantasy transport make airborne/amphibious national decapitation possible; this creates meaningful rear defence and a potentially severe positive-feedback cascade.
- Kender taunt documented as forced action rather than a passive bonus: an adjacent enemy stack can be compelled to attack alone and at a penalty before its chosen sequence.
- Standard asymmetry documented: concentrated Highlord aggressor with dragons and draconian production versus initially inactive Whitestone coalition with political growth, threat mobilisation, and later narrative counters.
- Victory documented as graded country control after thirty turns, with local scenario objectives; the role-playing quest supplies a second victory channel only when DL11 is actually linked to AD&D.
- Advanced system documented from *Dragon* 107: roughly monthly turns, active heroes, heroic actions, artefact search and uncovering, diplomacy, personal combat/capture, command radius, racial command, supply, interception, detailed winter, dragon-to-ship combat, and three new scenarios.
- Advanced agency shift documented: standard random activation/events yield partly to one active hero per side, on-map diplomacy, artefact conditions, and exceptional interventions.
- Advanced diplomacy risk documented: a hero can activate, deactivate, or reverse a national alliance, providing strong geographic agency but making a single check capable of outweighing extended military play.
- Advanced command documented: a leader’s tactical rating becomes a hex radius and armies require eligible national/racial command at the start of movement or combat.
- Advanced supply documented with its central loophole: only stacks of two or more armies trace to friendly fortified sites; lone armies forage, so dispersal can evade the intended constraint.
- Advanced interception and winter documented: six-hex naval/air reaction attempts, frozen rivers, ship-sinking risk, closed passes, blocked supply, shorter reaction range, and repeated flight-grounding checks.
- Advanced physical burden documented: the article requires additional or hand-copied counters and reconciles replacements for standard rules rather than providing a turnkey second edition.
- Official errata documented: ten counters lacked reverse-side statistics and the article supplied them; it also added leader fate. No comprehensive response to the later independent omission list was located.
- Simplicity classified into three categories: deliberate abstraction, productive compression, and genuine under-specification. Missing answers are not treated as optional depth.
- Contemporary *White Dwarf* review inspected directly: attractive counters and easy basic procedure, but thin maps, counter-key problems, unclear retreat/advance interaction, a moving-capital scenario exploit, poor organisation, weak fantasy feel, and heavy campaign-integration bookkeeping.
- Contemporary *Space Gamer/Fantasy Gamer* review inspected directly: appealing watercolour map, counters, readable rules, low price, and *Divine Right* lineage, followed by more than twenty reported serious omissions and several scenario-two errors after one scenario and two campaigns; the reviewer shelved it pending comprehensive errata.
- Physical interface finding retained: strong whole-continent visual orientation coexists with counter identification, stacking, setup-list, multiple-dice, distributed-reference, and adjudication friction.
- Campaign integration documented as primarily one-way: monthly army positions can generate news, refugees, encounters, and battles in AD&D; systematic feedback from character action into strategic state is mostly left to the referee.
- Solo boundary documented: the prologue allows solo play but provides no bot, decision table, or hidden-plan system; it is two-handed history generation.
- Balance evidence bounded: no win rates or developed-play corpus. Structural Highlord early advantages and Whitestone counterweights were recorded alongside activation, initiative, event, conquest, and advanced-diplomacy volatility.
- Afterlife evidence bounded: museum/archive preservation, current PDF record, a VASSAL implementation, a physical setup thread, fond and adverse recollections, and an unfinished 2026 digital hobby project demonstrate small persistent attachment, not representative replay.
- SSI lineage verified: both designs share the whole-continent factions, coalition opening, neutral nations, political access, force categories, terrain differences, initiative, replacement, conquest, draconians, magical reversals, campaign/history starts, and decisive geography.
- SSI divergence documented: square map, named diplomat allocation, alignment/alliance model, declarations, automated champion quests, discoveries, subversion, operation points, fatigue, quality, reconnaissance, long phase structure, setup handicaps, automated resolution, and Highlord AI.
- Credit boundary preserved: SSI credits David Landrey with design/programming, Chuck Kroegel with initial design/graphics, and Bret Berry with development; Niles and Hickman are not credited as computer designers.
- Central lineage finding: DL11 is the direct tabletop design precursor and strategic substrate of SSI’s licensed *War of the Lance*, while the computer title is a substantial adaptation/reworking rather than a literal port.
- Central design finding: a compelling strategic skeleton—politics changing geography, temporal coalition asymmetry, fantasy mobility, compressed attrition, and alternate history—was materially compromised by under-finished rules and components.
