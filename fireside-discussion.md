# Cross-game fireside discussion

**Status:** Deferred until the case-study corpus is judged sufficiently broad.  
**Purpose:** Capture Barry's own experience across the collection before conducting any design audit.

This will be a conversation, not a title-by-title questionnaire. Its main value is contrast: why one game survived dozens of hours while another, perhaps with individually admirable features, lost its place after thirty minutes.

## Evidence rules

- Start with free recollection. Do not lead with the dossiers' theories or a checklist of mechanics.
- Preserve Barry's direct recollections as personal evidence; distinguish them from later joint interpretation.
- Treat attraction and retention as different phenomena. A feature may sell the first session without sustaining the fifth.
- Treat admiration and enjoyment as different phenomena. A design may be clever, beautiful, or historically convincing yet still go unplayed.
- Treat redeeming features and decisive blockers separately. A failed game need not be wholly bad, and a favourite need not be frictionless.
- Consider context as evidence: edition, platform, age, available opponents, setup burden, rules access, session length, and competing demands may have shaped the experience.
- Actively seek counterexamples to every emerging pattern.
- Do not convert a recurring preference into a requirement for the new game during this discussion. That belongs to the later comparative audit.

## Conversation sequence

### 1. Unprompted landscape

Barry ranges freely across the collection: vivid memories, games that call for immediate praise or complaint, games repeatedly returned to, and games that simply faded. Record the language and contrasts that arise naturally before introducing analytical categories.

### 2. Contrastive exploration

Use Barry's own contrasts first. Where useful, compare games along provisional axes such as:

- immediate attraction versus long-term retention;
- decisions that felt consequential versus administration that felt like work;
- depth discovered through play versus complexity required before play;
- satisfying uncertainty versus loss of agency;
- asymmetry, changing roles, and narrative arc;
- tactical puzzles versus strategic direction;
- historical or fantastic theme as an active system versus decorative framing;
- setup, bookkeeping, interface, opponent, and session-length friction;
- replay driven by mastery, variation, story, competition, collection, or habit;
- terminal friction versus friction willingly tolerated because the return was high.

These are fallback lenses, not a script and not assumed truths.

### 3. Negative and mixed cases

For games that did not work, identify both what earned attention and what prevented continued play. Ask whether a blocker was intrinsic to the design, particular to an edition or platform, or contingent on Barry's circumstances at the time.

For favourites, identify blemishes that were tolerated, mitigated, house-ruled, or even transformed into part of the appeal.

### 4. Pattern challenge

Once tentative themes appear, look for:

- a loved game that violates an apparent preference;
- a disliked game that contains the supposedly desirable feature;
- two games with similar mechanics but different outcomes;
- changes in taste or circumstance over time;
- memories that conflict with the documented rules, reception, or edition history.

Contradictions are evidence to explain, not noise to smooth away.
